Clinical Infectious Diseases

Are Higher-Order Carbapenems Effective Therapy for Patients with Ertapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales Invasive Infections?

Peters AS, Diaz L, Martínez JRW et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Multicentre prospective cohort study (n=374), with IPW adjustment and genomic analysis.
Key Question: Do patients with ertapenem-resistant (ETP-R), non-carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales infections, treated with susceptible imipenem or meropenem, have worse outcomes than those with ertapenem-susceptible (ETP-S) ESBL/AmpC infections?
Key Findings:
  • After IPW, ETP-R infection (36% of cohort) was associated with higher 30-day mortality (OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.26-3.98); 14-day and in-hospital mortality were also higher.
  • Mortality was highest where isolates were resistant to two carbapenems (e.g. ertapenem and meropenem).
  • ETP-R isolates showed enrichment of porin alterations and additional non-carbapenemase β-lactamase genes (blaCTX-M, blaOXA-10).
Clinical Relevance: Findings challenge IDSA guidance that imipenem/meropenem is adequate for ETP-R, non-carbapenemase-producing isolates, so UK clinicians should be cautious about relying on in vitro susceptibility alone in this scenario.
Limitations: Observational design means residual confounding is possible despite IPW, and mechanistic explanations (partial hydrolysis) are inferred rather than demonstrated.

The Lancet Infectious Diseases

Combination antiviral treatment for a patient with lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus meningoencephalitis associated with bispecific antibody teclistamab therapy for myeloma

Maamari RG, Williams TGS, Zheng C et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Single-patient case report (UK).
Key Question: Can LCMV meningoencephalitis be identified and effectively treated in a profoundly immunocompromised patient on a bispecific antibody (teclistamab) for myeloma?
Key Findings:
  • Five months of progressive neurological illness (hearing loss, ataxia, headache, weight loss) with lymphocytic CSF, high protein and low glucose; conventional microbiology, autoimmune work-up and empirical antimicrobial/antituberculous therapy failed. CSF metagenomic sequencing identified LCMV; domestic rodent exposure was then elicited.
  • Ribavirin, remdesivir and molnupiravir were started (molnupiravir later switched to favipiravir, dosed by serum/CSF drug monitoring), continued for 6 months.
  • LCMV cleared from CSF and blood within 2 months; sustained neurological recovery, return to independence and work, with mild residual symptoms.
Clinical Relevance: LCMV is present in the UK (no published human cases since the 1950s) and should be considered in culture-negative encephalitis in immunocompromised patients, particularly those on T-cell-redirecting therapies, with metagenomic sequencing offering a diagnostic route.
Limitations: A single uncontrolled case cannot establish antiviral efficacy or attribute recovery to any specific agent, and the regimen's contribution is confounded by possible changes in immunosuppression.

Journal of Infection

The evolution of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in Greek intensive care units is driven by local carbapenemase acquisition, nationwide nosocomial spread and emerging resistance to novel antimicrobials

Baltas I, Vrioni G, Romero-Rodriguez OA et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Prospective multicentre molecular epidemiology study (isolate collection with phenotypic susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing), Greek ICUs, 2021–2023. Industry-funded (Pfizer).
Key Question: What are the transmission dynamics and residual treatment options for carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae* (CR-Kp) in Greek ICUs?
Key Findings:
  • Of 271 isolates, all carried carbapenemases (48% KPC, 20% NDM, 4.4% VIM, 27.6% multiple). Resistance exceeded 90% for carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides (except amikacin, 73.4%) and co-trimoxazole; colistin resistance was 46.5%.
  • Aztreonam-avibactam (94.5%) and cefepime-zidebactam (91.6%) were most active, but resistance exceeded 10% in multi-carbapenemase producers. Cefiderocol resistance was 41.7%, cefepime-taniborbactam 21.4%. Aztreonam-avibactam resistance co-occurred with VEB-25 and a novel VEB variant.
  • Over 88% of isolates belonged to transmission clusters spanning 1–8 ICUs; acquisition of additional carbapenemases after importation was common.
Clinical Relevance: Shows how rapidly carbapenemase diversification and resistance to newer agents can emerge, which is relevant to UK stewardship, screening of patients repatriated from high-prevalence settings, and diagnostic capability for VEB variants.

The Lancet Infectious Diseases

Ganaplacide plus lumefantrine solid dispersion formulation for children with uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria (KALUMI): an open-label, multicentre, randomised, controlled, adaptive, phase 2, non-inferiority trial

Fofana B, Zoleko-Manego R, Mombo-Ngoma G et al. · 2026 Nov 06
Study Type: Open-label, multicentre, randomised, adaptive, phase 2 non-inferiority trial (five African countries; run-in food-effect cohorts plus main efficacy cohorts)
Key Question: Is once-daily ganaplacide plus lumefantrine solid dispersion formulation (SDF) non-inferior to artemether-lumefantrine in children with uncomplicated *P. falciparum* malaria, and how does food affect drug exposure?
Key Findings:
  • PCR-corrected ACPR at day 29 was 99% (97/98) with ganaplacide-lumefantrine SDF vs 99% (98/99) with artemether-lumefantrine (difference -0.0%, 95% CI -2.9 to 2.8), meeting the 10% non-inferiority margin.
  • Food markedly increased lumefantrine exposure (fed:fasted ratios 1.48–2.87) but had little effect on ganaplacide, so a light meal was specified with dosing.
  • Vomiting (29% vs 11%) and hypokalaemia (25% vs 15%) were more frequent with the novel combination.
Clinical Relevance: A non-artemisinin-based combination is relevant to emerging artemisinin partial resistance in Africa, though use in UK practice will be as imported-malaria treatment once licensed.
Limitations: Open-label design with a small per-protocol population (n=197) in a phase 2 trial, limiting precision on safety signals (vomiting, hypokalaemia); phase 3 data are needed.

Emerging Infectious Diseases

Characteristics of and Clinical Criteria for Oropouche Virus Disease, United States, May 2024-January 2025

Platt L, Sutter RA, Morrison A et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Retrospective cross-sectional analysis of CDC surveillance and testing data (US travel-associated cases).
Key Question: Which symptoms and symptom combinations best distinguish Oropouche virus disease from other illness in patients tested during the 2024-25 Americas outbreak?
Key Findings:
  • Among 104 confirmed cases (median age 52 years; 52% male), the most common features were fever (94%), headache (83%) and myalgia (78%); 65 patients reported five or more symptoms.
  • Compared with 266 test-negative patients, headache, myalgia, fatigue/malaise and diarrhoea were associated with higher odds of Oropouche after adjusting for fever (p<0.05).
  • "Fever or chills, or two or more other symptoms" gave 99% sensitivity but only 7.5% specificity; adding travel history raised specificity to 61%.
Clinical Relevance: Symptoms alone cannot reliably discriminate Oropouche from other febrile illness in returning travellers, so UK testing criteria should combine clinical features with travel history to Oropouche-affected regions.
Limitations: Test-negative patients were a selected group referred for testing, so symptom comparisons and predictive values may not generalise to unselected febrile travellers.

Journal of Infection

Linezolid Minimum Inhibitory Concentration Creep and Precision Dosing in Older Adults: a longitudinal surveillance and multicentre population pharmacokinetic study

Liu T, Yuan Y, Wu J et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Longitudinal surveillance study with multicentre population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modelling and Monte Carlo simulation (four Chinese centres).
Key Question: How is linezolid susceptibility in *S. aureus* changing, and how should dosing be individualised in adults aged ≥65 years given age- and renal-related exposure variability?
Key Findings:
  • Across 506 isolates (2023-2026), geometric mean MIC rose from 1.587 to 1.796 mg/L; isolates at 2 mg/L increased from 69.2% to 82.2%, while those ≤1 mg/L fell from 30.8% to 16.7%.
  • PopPK (406 patients, 1,032 samples): age and eGFR influenced clearance.
  • At MIC 2 mg/L, target attainment was 82.0-95.3%, with 13.8-27.4% of simulated patients exceeding trough 16 mg/L; at MIC 4 mg/L, attainment was 72.0-94.5%, with 31.6-66.1% above this toxicity threshold.
Clinical Relevance: Supports early TDM and haematological monitoring in older or renally impaired patients on linezolid, and cautions against dose escalation for MIC 4 mg/L isolates, where alternative agents should be considered.
Limitations: Findings derive from Chinese centres with simulation-based targets, so UK applicability is uncertain, and the abstract reports no clinical outcome data (e.g., efficacy or toxicity events).

Journal of Infectious Diseases

Immunity and vaccines for cryptosporidiosis

Dann SM, White AC, Castellanos-Gonzalez A · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Narrative review (inferred from title and abstract; methodology not stated and no original data reported).
Key Question: What is known about host immunity to *Cryptosporidium*, and what does this imply for the feasibility and design of an effective vaccine?
Key Findings:
  • Natural infection appears to confer protection against subsequent disease, supporting the biological plausibility of vaccination.
  • Both innate and adaptive immunity contribute to clearance and prevention of infection.
  • An ideal vaccine would need to engage both arms of the immune response, including intestinal mucosal immunity.
  • The abstract provides no quantitative data, candidate vaccine details, or trial results.
Clinical Relevance: No vaccine is currently available, but understanding protective immunity may inform future prevention strategies for immunocompromised patients and outbreak settings in the UK, where cryptosporidiosis remains a notable cause of waterborne and zoonotic gastroenteritis.
Limitations: The abstract is brief and descriptive, with no stated search methods or specific vaccine candidates, so the strength of evidence cannot be judged from the abstract alone.

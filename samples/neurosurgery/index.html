Journal of neurosurgery

Incidence and predictors of complications, readmission, and reoperation following endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery for pituitary adenomas

Pham J, Cote DJ, Kang KM et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective single-institution cohort study
Key Question: What is the incidence and predictors of complications, early readmission, and early reoperation after endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery (eTSS) for pituitary adenomas?
Key Findings:
  • Among 709 operations, surgical complications were low (CSF leak 4.2%, epistaxis 2.7%, mortality 0.3%); transient diabetes insipidus was the most common medical complication (8.7%).
  • Early readmission occurred in 10.2% (mainly delayed hyponatraemia, 4.2%) and early reoperation in 4.8% (mainly CSF leak, 2.5%).
  • Larger tumour diameter, firm tumour consistency, intraoperative CSF leak, and extended approach independently predicted complications.
Clinical Relevance: Provides UK neurosurgeons with granular, procedure-specific complication and readmission benchmarks to support informed consent, MDT risk-stratification, and service audit standards for pituitary surgery.
Limitations: Single-centre retrospective design limits generalisability and may not capture practice variation across NHS pituitary units.

Journal of neurosurgery

Predicting gross-total resection in skull base chondrosarcoma: a multi-institutional machine learning study

Zuluaga-Garcia JP, Rubino F, Ramirez Ferrer E et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective multi-institutional cohort study with machine learning model development and internal validation.
Key Question: Can preoperative anatomical and clinical variables predict likelihood of gross-total resection (GTR) in skull base chondrosarcoma, and which surgical approach optimises this?
Key Findings:
  • Overall GTR rate 56% (n=185); for petroclival tumours, GTR varied markedly by approach: 72.1% with endoscopic transpterygoid approach vs 25.7% with open surgery vs 41.7% with midline EEA (p<0.001).
  • Increasing ICA encasement strongly reduced GTR odds (OR 3.12–9.76 depending on angle), as did prior radiotherapy (OR 4.04), petroclival location (OR 4.72), and CN VII–VIII involvement (OR 2.82).
  • Best-performing model (generalised linear model) achieved AUC 0.838, accuracy 0.898, Brier score 0.19.
Clinical Relevance: Offers UK skull base teams a validated, anatomy-based preoperative nomogram to guide corridor selection and counsel patients on realistic GTR expectations, informing MDT decisions on surgical approach and adjuvant radiotherapy planning.
Limitations: Retrospective design across four specialist centres limits generalisability and introduces selection/approach bias not fully captured by modelling.

Journal of neurosurgery

Maternal and fetal treatment outcomes of pregnancy-related aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: a nationwide study

Pohjola A, Korhonen A, Verho L et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective population-based cohort study (nationwide registry linkage, Finland, 1987–2016)
Key Question: What are the maternal and fetal outcomes following aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage (aSAH) occurring during pregnancy?
Key Findings:
  • Of 31 pregnant women with aSAH, 28 were hospitalised and treated (75% surgical clipping, 14% endovascular, 11% both); all 3 prehospital deaths occurred before hospitalisation, with **no maternal or fetal deaths among hospitalised cases**.
  • Vasospasm occurred in 18% and delayed cerebral ischaemia in 11%; median 3-month mRS was 1 (IQR 0–2), indicating favourable functional recovery.
  • Delivery was predominantly by caesarean section (46% emergency, 29% elective); most aneurysm treatment (82%) occurred antenatally.
Clinical Relevance: Supports a proactive, multidisciplinary management strategy (early aneurysm securing plus planned CS) for pregnancy-related aSAH, reassuring UK neurosurgical and obstetric teams that good maternal-fetal outcomes are achievable with timely in-hospital care.
Limitations: Small sample size (28 hospitalised cases over 30 years) limits statistical power and generalisability, and prehospital deaths could not be analysed for treatment factors.

Journal of neurosurgery

Ketamine in neurosurgery: a historical perspective

Sinclair AL, Jelkin G, Zucca B et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Historical/narrative review (commentary-style)
Key Question: What is ketamine's historical trajectory in neurosurgical anaesthesia, and how should its safety profile—particularly regarding intracranial pressure—be reassessed for modern practice?
Key Findings:
  • Ketamine was developed as a safer PCP derivative, FDA-approved in 1970, and favoured in paediatric neurosurgery for rapid onset, airway-preserving properties, and smooth recovery ("dissociative anaesthesia").
  • Its clinical use declined due to newer anaesthetics, early (contested) concerns about raised ICP, and stigma from recreational misuse.
  • The paper argues these ICP concerns are largely misconceptions warranting reappraisal in light of contemporary evidence.
Clinical Relevance: For UK neurosurgical and neurocritical care teams, revisiting ketamine's safety profile is relevant given ongoing debates about sedation choices in head-injured and critically ill patients within NHS intensive care settings.
Limitations: As a historical/narrative review, it lacks primary data or systematic methodology, limiting evidence-based conclusions on ICP effects.

Journal of neurosurgery

Diagnostic yield and safety of Remebot robot-assisted stereotactic biopsy: a 496-case retrospective analysis of intracranial lesions

Wang K, Yan F, Wang Y et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective cohort/case series (single-institution, 496 patients)
Key Question: Does the Remebot robot-assisted stereotactic biopsy system provide safe, high-yield diagnostic sampling of intracranial lesions?
Key Findings:
  • Overall diagnostic yield 85.9% (426/496); high-grade glioma (32.7%) and hematological malignancies (20.4%) were the commonest diagnoses.
  • Deep lesion location (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.27–0.94) and younger age (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99) independently predicted nondiagnostic biopsy; sex, supra-/infratentorial location, and brainstem involvement were not significant.
  • Perioperative complication rate was low at 1.8% (9/496), mostly haemorrhage (77.8%), occurring exclusively in male patients with high-grade glioma.
Clinical Relevance: Provides real-world evidence supporting robotic stereotactic biopsy platforms as a cost-effective, workflow-efficient alternative to established frame-based/robotic systems, relevant to UK units considering robotic neurosurgical adoption amid NHS capital constraints.
Limitations: Single-centre retrospective design without comparator arm limits generalisability and causal inference regarding platform-specific advantages.

Journal of neurosurgery

Cortical wiring cost abnormalities in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis: implications for postoperative seizure recurrence

Zhu F, Tao B, Li Y et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective case-control cohort study with imaging-based classification modelling
Key Question: Are preoperative gray matter cortical wiring cost abnormalities associated with postoperative seizure recurrence in mTLE-HS patients?
Key Findings:
  • Ipsilateral temporal cortex showed reduced global wiring costs, with more extensive reduction in the non-seizure-free (NSF, n=20) group versus seizure-free (SF, n=27) group.
  • Local wiring cost abnormalities were diffusely distributed across sensorimotor and default mode networks, more pronounced in NSF patients, and independent of hippocampal subfield atrophy, epilepsy duration, or medication.
  • An SVM classifier using these metrics distinguished NSF from SF patients with AUC = 0.86.
Clinical Relevance: Preoperative structural connectivity markers could help UK epilepsy surgery MDTs better counsel patients on likely postoperative seizure outcomes and refine surgical candidacy assessment.
Limitations: Small single-centre cohort (n=47) without external validation limits generalisability of the SVM prediction model.

Journal of neurosurgery

Association between intraoperative parameters and pain reduction after radiofrequency rhizotomy in patients with trigeminal neuralgia

Erol G, Sozer A, Tufek OY et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Retrospective cohort analysis (prospectively maintained database)
Key Question: Which intraoperative parameters during percutaneous RF rhizotomy for trigeminal neuralgia predict long-term pain control?
Key Findings:
  • CSF outflow occurred in 70.3% of procedures (116/165) and correlated positively with immediate (ρ=0.418) and long-term (ρ=0.539) pain reduction (both p<0.001).
  • CSF outflow and lower prelesion impedance were independent predictors of achieving BNI ≤IIIa at final follow-up (median 19 months).
  • Longer lesioning duration and greater cumulative thermal dose were associated with *worse* outcomes, suggesting prolonged lesioning reflects difficult electrode placement rather than added benefit.
Clinical Relevance: For UK neurosurgical/functional pain teams performing percutaneous RF rhizotomy, these findings support prioritising accurate electrode placement (confirmed by CSF outflow and low impedance) over aggressive or prolonged lesioning to optimise durable pain relief.
Limitations: Single-centre retrospective design with moderate follow-up limits generalisability and causal inference.

Journal of neurosurgery

Reassessing the risk of hemorrhage progression in mild traumatic brain injury: the role of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy

Harland TA, Topp G, Prabhala T et al. · 2026 Jul 31
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study
Key Question: Does anticoagulant/antiplatelet ("blood thinner") use independently increase the risk of hemorrhage progression, surgical intervention, or mortality in mild TBI with intracranial haemorrhage, justifying automatic high-risk classification under current Brain Injury Guidelines?
Key Findings:
  • Radiographic progression correlated with haemorrhage severity (mBIG 1: 1.7%, mBIG 2: 6.8%, mBIG 3: 14.1%), but blood thinner use alone did not increase progression compared with non-users in matched haemorrhage categories.
  • Patients classified as high-risk solely due to blood thinner use had lower progression rates (11.9%) than those classified by haemorrhage severity (18.1%, p<0.001).
  • Warfarin was the only agent independently associated with increased progression (OR 1.93, p=0.01); mortality and surgical rates did not differ by blood thinner status overall.
Clinical Relevance: These findings support refining BIG criteria to reduce unnecessary admissions and repeat imaging for mild TBI patients on antiplatelets or DOACs, with implications for NHS bed capacity and resource allocation, while reserving heightened vigilance for warfarin.
Limitations: Single-centre retrospective design limits generalisability and may introduce unmeasured confounding (e.g., INR control, DOAC dosing/timing).

Identifying the Right Diagnosis of Diabetes in Death: Unlocking A Key to Understanding Type 1 Diabetes Pathogenesis

Jacobsen LM, Campbell-Thompson M, Wasserfall C et al. · 2026 Jul 22
Study Type: Commentary/perspective article.
Key Question: How can postmortem and organ-donor research improve accurate classification of diabetes type, particularly type 1 diabetes with atypical presentations?
Key Findings:
  • Clinical classification of diabetes (type 1 vs type 2 vs monogenic) is frequently inaccurate, especially in adults with overlapping features (e.g., obesity with autoimmunity).
  • nPOD-based histopathological and genetic data (insulin-negative islets, insulitis, C-peptide loss, type 1 diabetes genetic risk scores) improve diagnostic classification beyond clinical history alone.
  • Diabetes pathogenesis is proposed to exist on a spectrum rather than fitting rigid categories.
Clinical Relevance: Misclassification affects treatment choice, glycaemic control, and complication risk, underscoring the need for UK endocrinologists to consider histological/genetic markers when phenotyping ambiguous adult-onset diabetes cases.
Limitations: As a commentary drawing on postmortem donor cohorts, conclusions are based on limited antemortem clinical data and are not derived from prospective, generalisable clinical studies.

Effects of Menstrual Cycle on Insulin Sensitivity in Type 1 Diabetes: An Observational Study of Individuals Using an Automated Insulin Delivery System

Hossmann S, Friedman M, Lizoain A et al. · 2026 Jul 22
Study Type: Observational cohort study (decentralised, real-world data)
Key Question: Does insulin sensitivity and glycaemic control vary across menstrual cycle phases in people with type 1 diabetes using automated insulin delivery (AID) systems?
Key Findings:
  • 77 participants contributed 380 cycles; total daily insulin dose and carbohydrate intake peaked in the luteal phase, with higher mean glucose and reduced time in range.
  • Bayesian modelling showed greater insulin sensitivity in early follicular phase (+2.6%, CrI 0.3–5.0) versus midluteal phase (−2.6%, CrI −5.2 to −0.1).
  • 84.7% of participants followed the population-level trend, but substantial interindividual variability was noted.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights that current AID algorithms and NHS diabetes management guidance do not account for cyclical insulin sensitivity changes, suggesting scope for menstrual-cycle-informed dosing adjustments or algorithm development to improve glycaemic outcomes in menstruating patients.
Limitations: Self-selected, decentralised sample with self-reported cycle phases limits generalisability and introduces potential reporting bias.

Fracture Risk Is Increased in Type 1 Diabetes and Is the Highest in Individuals With Diabetic Kidney Disease

Feodoroff M, Mäkimattila S, Thorn LM et al. · 2026 Jul 27
Study Type: Cohort study (registry-based, FinnDiane cohort with matched non-diabetic controls)
Key Question: How does fracture risk vary by age, sex, and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) stage in people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) compared with the general population?
Key Findings:
  • Overall fracture risk was more than doubled in T1D (SIR 2.15, 95% CI 2.06–2.25), with hip fracture risk over fivefold higher (SIR 5.24, 4.55–6.02), peaking in those aged 40–49 (SIR 8.13).
  • Fracture risk rose progressively with DKD stage, from SIR 2.54 (no albuminuria) to 5.08 (kidney failure) for any fracture, and 2.62 to 5.85 for major osteoporotic fractures.
  • Poor glycaemic control (HbA1c HR 1.11), hypoglycaemic coma (HR 1.04), and diabetic ketoacidosis episodes (HR 1.18) were independently associated with fracture risk.
Clinical Relevance: UK endocrinology services should consider earlier and more proactive fracture risk assessment in T1D patients, particularly those with albuminuria or declining renal function, rather than waiting until standard osteoporosis screening age thresholds.
Limitations: Observational design limits causal inference, and findings in this Finnish cohort may not fully generalise to more ethnically diverse UK populations.

Fulminant Secondary HLH Following EBV Reactivation in an Adult With Trisomy 21 After Teplizumab Treatment for Stage 2 Type 1 Diabetes

Klemann C, Kordonouri O · 2026 Jul 28
Study Type: Case report
Key Question: Can teplizumab treatment for stage 2 type 1 diabetes trigger severe EBV reactivation and secondary HLH, particularly in patients with trisomy 21?
Key Findings:
  • A 44-year-old man with trisomy 21 developed multi-organ failure 6 days post-teplizumab, with EBV PCR >2,000,000 copies/mL and biopsy-confirmed EBV-associated lymphoproliferation.
  • Secondary HLH criteria were met (HScore 238); dexamethasone plus four weekly rituximab doses achieved clinical, biochemical, and virologic recovery.
  • At 12 months, the patient remained insulin-free with stable stage 2 T1D.
Clinical Relevance: As teplizumab becomes more widely considered for at-risk T1D patients in the UK, clinicians should be aware of this rare but life-threatening complication and consider baseline EBV screening plus vigilance for HLH after treatment, especially in immunologically vulnerable groups such as trisomy 21.
Limitations: Single case report without pretreatment EBV PCR data, precluding causal attribution and generalisability.

Emergency Hospitalization, Amputation, and Survival After Charcot Neuro-Osteoarthropathy Diagnosis in People With Diabetes: A Regional Registry-Based Cohort Study

Milbourn VEL, Barn R, Tieges Z et al. · 2026 Jul 28
Study Type: Retrospective registry-based cohort study
Key Question: What are the rates and predictors of emergency hospitalisation, amputation, and mortality following first diagnosis of Charcot neuro-osteoarthropathy (CNO) in adults with diabetes?
Key Findings:
  • Among 140 adults with CNO (median follow-up 4.6 years), 87.9% had an emergency admission, 31.4% underwent lower-extremity amputation, and 37.9% died; 5-year survival was 67%, amputation-free survival 44%.
  • High/active foot-risk status independently predicted amputation (SHR 8.52, 95% CI 2.05–35.45) and shorter amputation-free survival (HR 2.09, 95% CI 1.15–3.80).
  • Advanced CKD (stages 4–5) increased risk across all outcomes; older age independently predicted mortality (HR 3.04, 95% CI 1.50–6.16).
Clinical Relevance: CNO should be recognised as a marker of high systemic vulnerability warranting prioritised, proactive multidisciplinary foot and renal surveillance within NHS diabetic foot pathways, given the substantial short-term risks of hospitalisation, amputation, and death.
Limitations: Small single-region cohort (140 patients) limits generalisability and statistical precision for subgroup analyses.

Diagnostic Tools to Establish the Presence of Peripheral Arterial Disease in People With Diabetes (DMPAD): A Multicenter Comparative Diagnostic Accuracy Study

Normahani P, Burgess L, Graham C et al. · 2026 Jul 29
Study Type: Prospective multicenter diagnostic accuracy study
Key Question: How accurate are commonly used bedside tests (ABPI, exercise ABPI, TBPI, visual and audible Doppler) for diagnosing PAD in people with diabetes, compared with CTA/MRA?
Key Findings:
  • Across 536 evaluable participants (37% with confirmed PAD on imaging), all bedside tests showed poor sensitivity: audible Doppler 36% (95% CI 27–45), visual Doppler 42% (33–51), ABPI 41% (32–50), exercise ABPI 41% (31–51), and TBPI 55% (46–64, best performing but still inadequate).
  • No bedside test reached clinically acceptable sensitivity thresholds for ruling out PAD.
Clinical Relevance: These UK multicentre data challenge reliance on standard bedside vascular assessment in diabetic foot pathways, suggesting NHS services should lower thresholds for imaging referral when PAD is clinically suspected.
Limitations: Sensitivity findings depend on a single stenosis threshold (≥50%) and ANGIO scoring system, which may not capture the full clinical spectrum of PAD severity.

In Utero Exposure to Preexisting or Gestational Diabetes Mellitus Increases Incidence of Diabetes in the Children of First Nations and Non-First Nations Mothers: A Retrospective Cohort Study

Shah BR, Odugbemi T, Li Z et al. · 2026 Jul 31
Study Type: Retrospective population-based cohort study
Key Question: Does in-utero exposure to maternal preexisting or gestational diabetes increase a child's risk of developing diabetes, and does periconception glycaemic control modify this risk?
Key Findings:
  • Among 2,851,583 children (median follow-up age 16 years), maternal preexisting diabetes markedly increased offspring diabetes risk (non-First Nations HR 5.18 [4.82–5.57]; First Nations HR 7.68 [5.91–9.98]).
  • Gestational diabetes exposure also raised risk (HR 1.75 [1.65–1.84] non-First Nations; 2.59 [2.07–3.24] First Nations).
  • Each 1% (11 mmol/mol) reduction in periconception HbA1c was associated with an 11% lower offspring diabetes risk (HR 0.89 [0.80–0.995], P=0.04).
Clinical Relevance: Supports prioritising preconception glycaemic optimisation in women with diabetes across UK maternity/diabetes services, with particular attention to intergenerational risk in Indigenous and potentially other high-risk populations.
Limitations: Observational design using administrative data limits causal inference and control for unmeasured confounders (e.g., shared genetic/lifestyle factors, postnatal environment).

Safeguarding the development of offspring: the role of genetic integrity of the maternal germline

Cai X, Hutt KJ, Winship AL et al. · 2026 Jul 22
Study Type: Narrative review
Key Question: How does maternal genetic and genomic integrity (maternal effect genes and de novo variants) influence embryonic development and offspring health across generations?
Key Findings:
  • Maternal effect genes (MEGs) regulate epigenetic reprogramming, mitochondrial function, DNA repair, and cell division during early zygotic development; the review compiles a curated MEG reference spanning mouse models and human pathogenic variants.
  • Maternally derived de novo variants (DNVs), arising from replication/repair errors in the germline, contribute to a broader-than-recognised range of sporadic disorders; the authors present the first comprehensive disease catalogue linking maternal DNVs to offspring phenotypes.
  • Maternal age and mutational mechanisms (recombination errors, epigenetic drift) modulate DNV transmission risk, with implications for multigenerational disease patterns and evolutionary outcomes.
Clinical Relevance: For UK endocrinologists managing fertility, reproductive endocrine disorders, and counselling on maternal age-related risks, this review provides a mechanistic framework and diagnostic reference linking maternal germline integrity to offspring disease, informing genetic counselling and ART-related risk assessment.
Limitations: As a narrative review, it synthesises existing mouse and human data without new primary evidence or systematic quality appraisal, limiting definitive clinical guidance.

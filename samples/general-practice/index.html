British Journal of General Practice

Are non-cancer diagnoses missed opportunities in diagnosing cancer? Observational case-control studies

Price S, Abel GA, Black G et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Observational matched case-control and case-only analyses using registry-linked CPRD primary care data (English patients; 223,422 included).
Key Question: Which non-cancer diagnoses made in the year before cancer diagnosis are associated with cancer and with better or worse outcomes, and so may represent missed diagnostic opportunities?
Key Findings:
  • 37 "interim diagnoses" were identified across 7 cancer sites: 18 consistently associated with worse outcomes (stage, route to diagnosis, 1-year survival), 8 with better outcomes, and 11 with mixed associations.
  • Example: GORD, gastritis, oesophagitis or peptic ulcer were linked to worse outcomes in pancreatic, colorectal and ovarian cancers, better outcomes in stomach cancer, and mixed outcomes in oesophageal cancer.
  • Specific effect sizes and confidence intervals are not reported in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: Common benign diagnoses that plausibly explain red-flag symptoms may delay cancer recognition, supporting targeted safety-netting and structured follow-up of unresolved symptoms in NHS primary care.
Limitations: Observational design cannot establish causation or confirm true diagnostic delay; the abstract gives no effect sizes, so clinical magnitude is unclear.

British Journal of General Practice

Molecular bacterial detection in nasopharyngeal swabs does not predict the clinical course of lower respiratory tract infections in primary care: an ancillary study of a randomized controlled trial

Knüsli J, Lhopitallier L, Kronenberg A et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Secondary (ancillary) analysis of a multicentre RCT, with retrospective PCR testing of stored samples.
Key Question: Does PCR detection of bacteria in nasopharyngeal swabs predict a worse clinical course in adults with lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in primary care?
Key Findings:
  • Bacteria were detected in 142/345 analysed patients (41%). Median time to recovery was 7 days in both groups (95% CI 6–8; p=0.29).
  • Adjusted HR for recovery with bacterial detection was 1.13 (95% CI 0.84–1.50). The interaction with antibiotic prescription was also null (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.64–1.90).
  • Durations of cough, sputum and dyspnoea did not differ by bacterial status; median cough duration was 16 days (95% CI 15–18).
Clinical Relevance: Nasopharyngeal bacterial PCR does not appear to identify patients likely to do worse, so point-of-care molecular testing is unlikely to guide antibiotic decisions for LRTI in UK general practice.
Limitations: Only 345 of 469 randomised patients were analysed, and the study was not powered for this question, so modest or subgroup effects (e.g. in higher-risk patients) cannot be excluded.

The BMJ

Interventions for adhesive capsulitis: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials

Meng J, Liu P, Gomes NS et al. · 2026 Nov 04
Study Type: Systematic review and network meta-analysis of RCTs (189 trials, 12,988 participants, 59 interventions).
Key Question: Which interventions are most effective and safe for adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)?
Key Findings:
  • Short term, extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) probably reduces pain versus usual care (MD -1.97/10, 95% CI -2.97 to -0.97; moderate certainty). Functional benefit was low certainty with important incoherence.
  • Short-term flexion probably improves with manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) plus intra-articular steroid (33.3°, 95% CI 11.2 to 55.4) or MUA alone (26.2°, 8.2 to 44.1); both moderate certainty.
  • Most other effects, all mid-term effects and comparative safety were low or very low certainty; adverse event reporting was inconsistent.
Clinical Relevance: Evidence supporting any single treatment for frozen shoulder is weak, which is relevant to UK primary care decisions on physiotherapy, steroid injection and secondary care referral; ESWT and MUA are not routinely available in many NHS settings.
Limitations: Around 71% of trials contributing to the main outcomes were at high risk of bias, with predominantly short-term follow-up and uncertain applicability beyond primary adhesive capsulitis.

British Journal of General Practice

Difficulties with early prostate cancer diagnosis: a multi-perspective mixed-methods study

Hiscock J, Formby L, Din N et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Mixed-methods study (descriptive questionnaires plus thematic analysis of interviews and focus groups), Wales, UK.
Key Question: How do patients, GPs and urologists experience the early diagnostic pathway for prostate cancer, and where could earlier or more equitable diagnosis be supported?
Key Findings:
  • Questionnaires: 210 patients, 65 GPs, 69 urologists. Qualitative component: 24 patients, 18 GPs, 11 secondary care clinicians.
  • Patients reported delayed symptom recognition, difficulty accessing appointments, repeat presentations, and waits for diagnostic tests.
  • GPs described managing diagnostic uncertainty and variable access to investigations; urologists cited resource and referral pathway constraints. All groups attributed delays to system-wide factors, not primary care alone.
Clinical Relevance: Findings support consistent local pathways and equitable access to diagnostic tests (e.g. MRI), and caution against attributing diagnostic delay to primary care alone in a context without routine screening.
Limitations: The abstract gives no quantitative data on time intervals or sequence of events despite the stated aim of mapping them, and findings from a single UK nation may not be generalisable.

Journal of General Internal Medicine

Stakeholder Perceptions for the Development of Gabapentinoid Deprescribing Clinical Guideline: A Functional Role Theory Approach

Cheng CHJ, Gnjidic D, Mathieson S et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Exploratory qualitative study (semi-structured interviews; reflexive thematic analysis informed by functional role theory).
Key Question: What do patients and healthcare professionals expect from, and see as the scope of, a gabapentinoid deprescribing guideline?
Key Findings:
  • 25 participants (13 consumers, 12 HCPs including GPs, pharmacists, neurologists) viewed deprescribing as a system-wide task involving allied health professionals, not prescribers alone.
  • Role ambiguity was prominent: prescribers initiating gabapentinoids were often separate from those expected to deprescribe, leaving GPs managing long-term use. Both GPs and patients reported navigating tapering without formal guidance, resulting in fragmented care.
  • HCPs favoured a "prescribing continuum" over separating prescribing from deprescribing. Consumers and carers wanted to be central to both guideline development and the deprescribing process.
Clinical Relevance: GPs commonly inherit long-term gabapentinoid prescriptions started elsewhere, and this study supports the need for shared accountability, coordinated care and co-designed tapering guidance in UK practice.
Limitations: Small, self-selected sample with likely non-UK setting (generalisability to NHS primary care is uncertain), and the study explores perceptions only, offering no tapering recommendations or outcome data.

The BMJ

Perioperative respiratory adverse events in children undergoing adenotonsillectomy with laryngeal mask airway versus endotracheal tube ventilation: multicentre, randomised controlled trial

Han Y, Yi X, Xia J et al. · 2026 Nov 04
Study Type: Multicentre, parallel-group RCT (n=400, four tertiary hospitals in China).
Key Question: Does laryngeal mask airway (LMA) ventilation reduce perioperative respiratory adverse events (PRAEs) compared with endotracheal tube (ETT) in children aged 2-6 years undergoing adenotonsillectomy?
Key Findings:
  • Any PRAE (ITT): 22.5% LMA vs 57.5% ETT (absolute risk difference -35.0 percentage points, 95% CI -43.5 to -25.7; P<0.001).
  • Benefit was largest postoperatively (16.5% vs 49.5%), driven by less severe cough (10.5% vs 42.0%), desaturation (15.0% vs 28.0%) and airway obstruction (3.5% vs 12.5%). Laryngospasm occurred only with ETT (3.0% vs 0%).
  • LMA-to-ETT conversion occurred in three children; no serious adverse events in either group.
Clinical Relevance: Findings are relevant to ENT surgical pathways for paediatric tonsillectomy, where GPs and ACPs see postoperative respiratory complications and manage parental concerns, though airway choice remains an anaesthetic decision.
Limitations: Single-country setting with experienced anaesthetists at tertiary centres, and anaesthetists could not be blinded, so generalisability to other settings is uncertain.

The BMJ

Thoracic epidural versus paravertebral blockade for reducing chronic post-thoracotomy pain (TOPIC-2): open label, parallel group, superiority, multicentre, randomised controlled trial

Shelley B, Middleton L, Boyles R et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Open-label, multicentre (15 UK thoracic centres), parallel-group, superiority RCT (n=737 analysed).
Key Question: Does paravertebral blockade reduce chronic post-thoracotomy pain at six months compared with thoracic epidural?
Key Findings:
  • Chronic pain (VAS ≥40 mm) at six months: 22% paravertebral vs 16% epidural (adjusted RR 1.32, 95% CI 0.93–1.86; p=0.12). Superiority was not shown.
  • Day 1 worst pain was 7.7 mm higher with paravertebral (95% CI 2.8–12.5), with no difference on days 2–3.
  • Paravertebral had less hypotension (12% vs 18%) and pruritus (4% vs 12%) but more urinary retention (12% vs 6%; RR 2.21, 1.35–3.61).
Clinical Relevance: Chronic post-surgical pain remains common after thoracotomy; this trial supports either technique for UK thoracic units, so GPs should expect a significant minority of patients to need ongoing pain review regardless of technique.
Limitations: Open-label design with only 564 of 737 participants contributing primary outcome data, so attrition and unblinded reporting may bias results, and the trial cannot exclude a modest harm from paravertebral blockade.

British Journal of General Practice

"It does feel different when you're seeing a colleague": A qualitative study of general practitioners' experiences of treating doctor-patients

Hettiarachchi D, Bradfield O, Tayal A et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Qualitative study (semi-structured interviews, reflexive thematic analysis); 22 GPs in Australia.
Key Question: What are GPs' experiences of treating doctor-patients, and what could improve care and help-seeking among doctors?
Key Findings:
  • Barriers to doctors seeking care were practical, professional and psychological; GPs felt these could be eased by acknowledging patient expertise while maintaining boundaries, remaining thorough, and clarifying expectations.
  • Mandatory reporting was rarely needed and could support recovery in impaired doctors, but risked undermining the therapeutic alliance.
  • GPs advocated a professional culture that values GP care, defines the regular GP's role and normalises help-seeking; many found treating doctor-patients rewarding.
Clinical Relevance: Doctors' elevated burnout, suicide and substance-use risk makes this relevant to UK practice, where GPs also treat colleagues and navigate GMC reporting duties, though findings need cautious transfer to the NHS context.
Limitations: Single-country, GP-only sample (Australian regulatory and healthcare context), with no doctor-patient perspectives, limiting transferability to the UK.

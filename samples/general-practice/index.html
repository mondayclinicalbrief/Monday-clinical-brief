BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

Risk of hair loss associated with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in adults with type 2 diabetes: target trial emulation

Tang H, Zhang B, Lu Y et al. · 2026 Jul 22
Study Type: Target trial emulation (retrospective observational cohort using electronic health records).
Key Question: Is GLP-1 receptor agonist use associated with increased risk of alopecia compared with SGLT-2 or DPP-4 inhibitors in adults with type 2 diabetes?
Key Findings:
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with higher alopecia risk than SGLT-2 inhibitors (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.73) and DPP-4 inhibitors (HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.28–2.20).
  • Association was specific to non-scarring alopecia (HR 1.53 vs SGLT-2i; HR 1.72 vs DPP-4i) and remained consistent across sensitivity/subgroup analyses, though attenuated after negative control calibration.
Clinical Relevance: With rising GLP-1 receptor agonist prescribing in NHS diabetes and weight-management pathways, clinicians should be aware of a modest but statistically significant increased risk of non-scarring hair loss to inform counselling and shared decision-making.
Limitations: Observational design based on diagnostic codes risks residual confounding and misclassification despite adjustment and negative control methods.

BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

Minimal detectable change of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Patient Health Questionnaire-8, and Patient Health Questionnaire-2: individual patient data meta-analysis

Wang Y, Wu Y, González-Domínguez NP et al. · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Individual participant data meta-analysis (94–98 studies, >42,000 participants).
Key Question: What change in PHQ-9, PHQ-8, and PHQ-2 scores exceeds measurement error and can be considered clinically meaningful change, and does this vary by setting or population?
Key Findings:
  • Pooled MDC95 (95% confidence threshold) was 5.72 points (95% CI 5.54–5.90) for PHQ-9, 5.51 (5.33–5.68) for PHQ-8, and 2.26 (2.15–2.37) for PHQ-2.
  • MDC95 for PHQ-9 was higher in inpatient settings (6.48, 95% CI 6.05–6.92) and increased by 0.40 points per 10% rise in prevalence of major depression in the sample; age and sex had negligible effect.
  • Prediction intervals were wide (e.g., PHQ-9: 4.00–7.44), indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity.
Clinical Relevance: For UK general practice, where PHQ-9 is widely used to monitor depression (e.g., IAPT, QOF), a ≥6-point change is a reasonable threshold to confidently interpret as real clinical improvement or deterioration rather than measurement noise; higher thresholds may be needed in specialist mental health settings.
Limitations: Wide prediction intervals reflect considerable heterogeneity across studies/settings, limiting precision of a single universal threshold.

BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

Early treatment with rasburicase and risk of kidney replacement therapy and death in adults with tumour lysis syndrome: emulated target trial

Shenoy T, Sanchez-Almanzar D, Dias JA et al. · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Emulated target trial (observational cohort with inverse probability of treatment weighting)
Key Question: Does rasburicase given within 12 hours of tumour lysis syndrome (TLS) onset reduce acute kidney injury requiring kidney replacement therapy or death compared with delayed/no treatment?
Key Findings:
  • Early rasburicase (median 5.0 hours after TLS onset) was associated with lower composite risk of kidney replacement therapy or death (32.7% vs 42.0%; adjusted OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.88, P<0.001).
  • 90-day mortality was also reduced (adjusted OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.94), with consistent findings across sensitivity analyses.
  • Effect held despite treated patients having higher baseline uric acid concentrations.
Clinical Relevance: Supports prompt rasburicase administration in patients with suspected TLS (e.g., in oncology/haematology shared-care or acute admissions), reinforcing early recognition and rapid escalation pathways relevant to GPs referring patients with high tumour burden malignancies.
Limitations: Observational design with residual confounding despite weighting, as treatment allocation was not randomised.

Identifying clinical signs of child abuse in primary care: a systematic review

Corsi N, Van der Windt N, Menage Q et al. · 2026 Jun 11
Study Type: Systematic review
Key Question: What clinical signs, presenting in primary care, should prompt clinicians to consider child abuse?
Key Findings:
  • 19 studies (from 3811 screened, 1996–2024) identified; physical signs included fractures, bruises, burns, and genital/neurological injuries.
  • Non-specific presentations were common: abdominal pain, persistent unexplained symptoms, atopic conditions (eczema, asthma), depression, anxiety, and despair.
  • Behavioural disturbances (externalising/internalising) and developmental delays (language, learning, social interaction) were also associated with abuse.
Clinical Relevance: GPs and ACPs should maintain a high index of suspicion for abuse when children present repeatedly with unexplained somatic, atopic, behavioural, or developmental concerns, supporting earlier safeguarding referral in line with NHS child protection guidance.
Limitations: Evidence base is limited (only 19 studies), heterogeneous in design, and findings are largely associative rather than diagnostic, limiting specificity for clinical decision-making.

Artificial intelligence-based automated scoring of the Clock Drawing Test in older adults in primary care: association with Mini-Mental State Examination scores, discriminative performance, and agreement with clinician rating

Tutu ÖG, Bilen V, Ziroğlu Z · 2026 Jun 11
Study Type: Cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study
Key Question: Can AI-based automated scoring of the Clock Drawing Test reliably substitute for clinician scoring in detecting cognitive impairment in older primary care patients?
Key Findings:
  • AI-based CDT scores showed moderate correlation with MMSE (ρ = .437, P < .001).
  • Manual scoring outperformed AI in discriminating cognitive impairment (AUC .779 vs .715, P = .028); AI had higher sensitivity but lower specificity.
  • Agreement between methods was good (ICC = .721), though AI scored slightly higher on average (mean difference 1.13).
Clinical Relevance: AI-assisted CDT scoring could support standardisation and efficiency in dementia screening within UK primary care, but should not replace clinician judgement.
Limitations: Single-centre cross-sectional design with clinician scoring as reference standard limits generalisability and introduces potential rater-dependent bias.

Pulmonary congestion assessed by lung ultrasound in stable ambulatory patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction in primary care

Leceaga-Gaztambide E, Domingo M, Manresa JM et al. · 2026 Jun 11
Study Type: Prospective multisite observational cohort study.
Key Question: Does lung ultrasound detect subclinical pulmonary congestion in clinically stable ambulatory HFpEF patients managed in primary care, and what does it correlate with?
Key Findings:
  • 86.5% of 188 stable HFpEF patients had ≥1 B-line on 28-zone lung ultrasound; one-third had ≥5 B-lines despite clinical stability.
  • Higher B-line counts correlated with higher systolic BP, crackles, and elevated NT-proBNP and CA-125.
  • No association found between B-line burden and 6-minute walk distance, frailty index, or HF-related quality of life.
Clinical Relevance: In UK primary care, where HFpEF follow-up relies heavily on symptoms and clinical review, lung ultrasound could identify occult congestion missed by standard assessment, potentially informing diuretic titration before decompensation.
Limitations: Cross-sectional design precludes conclusions on whether B-line-guided management improves outcomes or reduces hospitalisation.

From guidelines to practice: general practitioners' consensus on indicators for managing selected multimorbidity in primary care in Western Australia

Ishimwe SMC, Carey TA, Affandi JS et al. · 2026 Jun 11
Study Type: Delphi consensus study (two-round reactive Delphi survey)
Key Question: Can Australian GPs reach consensus on a unified set of indicators for guideline-adherent management of common multimorbid conditions (T2DM, obesity, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, CKD)?
Key Findings:
  • 61 of 65 candidate indicators reached consensus (≥70% of 21 GPs rating relevance ≥3) by Round 2, spanning biophysical, lifestyle, and pharmacological domains.
  • Two indicators on pharmacological management of CKD with coexisting T2DM lacked clarity and need refinement.
  • Qualitative feedback highlighted gaps in lipid management guidance, the importance of prioritising obesity prevention, and difficulties delivering lifestyle counselling.
Clinical Relevance: Offers a potential unified, practical tool to help UK GPs manage overlapping chronic conditions more holistically, addressing similar fragmentation seen in NICE single-disease guidelines and supporting more efficient multimorbidity reviews.
Limitations: Small, single-region (Western Australian) GP sample using Australian-specific guidelines limits generalisability and requires piloting before wider adoption.

Journal of general internal medicine

Comparative Effectiveness of Antidepressants and Second-Generation Antipsychotics for Agitation Among Nursing Home Residents with Dementia

Liang S, Lapane KL, Ott BR et al. · 2026 Jul 20
Study Type: Retrospective active comparator new-user cohort study (US Medicare claims + MDS 3.0 data)
Key Question: In nursing home residents with dementia-related agitation, are SSRIs more effective than second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) for reducing agitation?
Key Findings:
  • Among 19,508 SSRI and 21,541 SGA new users, SSRIs were associated with greater reduction in Agitated and Reactive Behavior Scale score (difference −0.14, 95% CI −0.19 to −0.09) versus SGAs.
  • SSRI users had a higher probability of achieving minimal clinically important improvement (+3.6%, 95% CI 2.7–4.6%).
  • Benefit was greatest in residents with mild-to-moderate agitation and mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment.
Clinical Relevance: Supports SSRIs as a potentially safer first-line pharmacological option over antipsychotics for managing agitation in dementia, relevant to UK prescribing practice given existing MHRA/NICE cautions on antipsychotic use in dementia and emphasis on minimising antipsychotic burden in care homes.
Limitations: Observational design using claims/MDS data limits causal inference despite propensity-score weighting, and effect sizes are modest and may not reflect clinically meaningful benefit for all patients.

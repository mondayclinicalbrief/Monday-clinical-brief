Annals of intensive care

Factors associated with treatment resumption and long-term outcomes after ICU discharge in patients with hematological malignancies

Messéant O, Guégan M, Painvin B et al. · 2026
Study Type: Single-centre retrospective observational cohort study
Key Question: What factors predict resumption of anticancer therapy and long-term survival after ICU discharge in patients with haematological malignancies?
Key Findings:
  • Of 171 ICU survivors, 119 had further treatment indications; 89.1% resumed anticancer therapy despite significant functional decline during ICU stay (median performance status change -1.0, 95%CI -1.0 to -1.0).
  • Cardiovascular disease reduced likelihood of treatment resumption (HR 0.41, 95%CI 0.20–0.84); malignancy diagnosed during ICU admission increased it (HR 3.03, 95%CI 1.53–6.01).
  • Five-year survival was 36.1%; mortality risk was lower with infection as ICU admission cause (HR 0.54), new HM diagnosis during ICU stay (HR 0.31), and certain HM subtypes (HR 0.40).
Clinical Relevance: Supports pragmatic, disease- and comorbidity-informed ICU admission and treatment decisions for UK haemato-oncology patients, reassuring clinicians that ICU survival does not preclude continued cancer treatment.
Limitations: Single-centre retrospective design limits generalisability and introduces potential selection and confounding bias.

Critical care (London, England)

Indirect calorimetry-guided nutrition during weaning from prolonged mechanical ventilation: a double-blind randomized controlled trial

Fradkin M, Elyashiv M, Brik M et al. · 2026 Aug 28
Study Type: Double-blind randomized controlled trial (single-centre, with post hoc per-protocol analysis)
Key Question: Does indirect calorimetry (IC)-guided nutrition improve weaning success and time to liberation from prolonged mechanical ventilation compared with predictive-equation-guided feeding?
Key Findings:
  • Successful weaning occurred in 58% (IC) vs 50% (control); median time to weaning 27 vs 31 days—neither difference statistically significant (p=0.33 and p=0.58).
  • One-year mortality was numerically lower with IC (48% vs 56%; absolute difference -8%, 95% CI -21.2 to 5.5%, p=0.29).
  • Smaller "energy-gap" (measured expenditure vs delivered intake) independently predicted successful weaning (HR 1.02 per 50 kcal/day reduction, p=0.02); successful weaning independently reduced 1-year mortality (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.36–0.82).
Clinical Relevance: Supports growing interest in metabolically-guided feeding for long-stay ventilated patients in UK critical care/rehabilitation units, though current evidence does not yet justify routine IC adoption over standard predictive equations.
Limitations: Single-centre design and underpowered sample size limit definitive conclusions; findings are hypothesis-generating pending larger multicentre trials.

Critical care (London, England)

The NLRP3 inflammasome in physiological and dysfunctional host response in human sepsis and critical illness: a narrative review

Neumann C, Hickl F, Kemnitzer J et al. · 2026 Sep 19
Study Type: Narrative review
Key Question: What role does NLRP3 inflammasome activation play in sepsis and critical illness, and how might this be therapeutically targeted?
Key Findings:
  • NLRP3 activation drives caspase-1-dependent IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and pyroptosis; excessive activation is linked to hyperinflammation, endothelial dysfunction, immunothrombosis, and multi-organ failure in sepsis/ARDS.
  • Conversely, exhausted or insufficient inflammasome responses may impair pathogen clearance and increase risk of secondary infection, indicating a biphasic, time-dependent role.
  • IL-1 blockade (e.g., anakinra) and upstream pathway inhibitors are proposed immunomodulatory strategies, but no definitive clinical efficacy data are presented in this review.
Clinical Relevance: Understanding the dual protective/pathogenic role of NLRP3 signalling may inform future biomarker-guided immunomodulatory therapies for sepsis and ARDS relevant to UK critical care practice, where phenotype-targeted treatment remains an unmet need.
Limitations: As a narrative review, it synthesises mechanistic and preclinical evidence without systematic methodology or robust clinical trial data supporting translation.

Critical care (London, England)

Dosing of anti-infective drugs in patients undergoing therapeutic plasma exchange - considerations for practical application: a narrative review

Nachtigall MB, Schmidt JJ, Scherneck S et al. · 2026 Sep 24
Study Type: Narrative review
Key Question: How does therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) affect the pharmacokinetics and dosing of anti-infective drugs in critically ill patients?
Key Findings:
  • TPE typically exchanges 1.0–1.5 plasma volumes over 90–140 minutes, but this varies considerably in practice.
  • Drugs with low volume of distribution and high protein binding (e.g., aminoglycosides, glycopeptides) are most susceptible to significant removal during TPE.
  • Direct pharmacokinetic evidence for anti-infectives during TPE in sepsis is limited, and no validated dosing/timing algorithms currently exist.
Clinical Relevance: With two multicentre trials evaluating TPE in early septic shock and TPE increasingly used in UK critical care for multiple indications, clinicians must anticipate potential subtherapeutic anti-infective levels and consider individualised dose adjustment or therapeutic drug monitoring around TPE sessions.
Limitations: As a narrative (non-systematic) review, it synthesises heterogeneous, largely low-quality pharmacokinetic data without formal quality appraisal, limiting the strength of practical dosing recommendations.

Critical care (London, England)

Increased ureagenesis revealing metabolic intolerance to enhanced nutrition in the PICU: a secondary analysis of the PEPaNIC RCT

Vanhorebeek I, Umman Serin N, Verbruggen SCAT et al. · 2026 Sep 25
Study Type: Secondary analysis of an RCT (PEPaNIC trial)
Key Question: Does increased ureagenesis (urea/urea-creatinine ratio) explain the harm associated with early parenteral nutrition (PN) in critically ill children?
Key Findings:
  • Early-PN patients had persistently higher urea and urea/creatinine ratio (UCR) than late-PN patients over the first 11 PICU days.
  • Higher week-1 maximum UCR independently predicted new infection (aOR 1.75, 95%CI 1.35–2.27), delayed PICU discharge (aHR 0.75, 0.67–0.82) and hospital discharge (aHR 0.78, 0.70–0.86), and increased 90-day mortality (aOR 1.43, 1.03–1.99), statistically mediating part of the harm from early-PN.
  • Similar associations held for absolute urea concentrations.
Clinical Relevance: Supports UK PICU practice of withholding early PN and suggests urea/UCR could become a bedside biomarker to individualise nutrition timing and dosing in critically ill children.
Limitations: This is an observational, hypothesis-generating secondary analysis showing statistical mediation, not proven causation, and requires prospective validation before clinical implementation.

Epilepsy After Antipsychotic Prescription in Survivors of Traumatic Brain Injury: A Population-Based Cohort Study

Ferraris A, Szmulewicz AG, Adhikari NKJ et al. · 2026 Sep 21
Study Type: Retrospective population-based cohort study
Key Question: Does the choice of antipsychotic (haloperidol or risperidone vs quetiapine) prescribed after TBI hospitalisation affect the risk of post-traumatic epilepsy in older adults?
Key Findings:
  • Among 1204 TBI survivors (age ≥66) prescribed antipsychotics post-discharge, epilepsy occurred in 6% (haloperidol), 13% (risperidone), and 14% (quetiapine).
  • No significant difference in adjusted epilepsy risk versus quetiapine: haloperidol aHR 0.73 (95% CI 0.39–1.34); risperidone aHR 0.94 (95% CI 0.65–1.36).
  • Five-year mortality was markedly higher with haloperidol (89%) than risperidone (66%) or quetiapine (62%), suggesting confounding by indication/frailty.
Clinical Relevance: For UK ICU/critical care clinicians managing agitation or delirium after TBI, this study offers reassurance that antipsychotic choice does not appear to meaningfully alter post-traumatic epilepsy risk, though drug selection should still consider sedation profile, cardiac risk, and mortality-associated confounders.
Limitations: Very high and differential mortality across groups, plus wide confidence intervals, limits comparability and statistical power, warranting cautious interpretation pending confirmatory studies.

A multicenter randomized, double-blinded placebo-controlled phase 2 trial to evaluate safety and efficacy of ilofotase alfa in patients at risk for kidney injury following open heart surgery

Mourisse L, Ostermann M, Thiessen S et al. · 2026 Sep 21
Study Type: Phase 2 multicentre randomised, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial
Key Question: Does perioperative ilofotase alfa reduce postoperative renal functional impairment in patients at high risk of acute kidney injury after on-pump cardiac surgery?
Key Findings:
  • No significant difference in peak serum creatinine ratio: 1.21±0.42 (ilofotase alfa) vs 1.27±0.50 (placebo), p=0.31.
  • MAKE60 incidence similar between groups: 15.9% vs 15.4% (p=0.87).
  • No safety concerns identified with ilofotase alfa administration.
Clinical Relevance: Cardiac surgery-associated AKI remains a common, morbidity-driving complication in NHS cardiothoracic ICUs, but this trial indicates ilofotase alfa offers no renoprotective benefit, tempering enthusiasm for its adoption pending further evidence.
Limitations: Phase 2 design with modest sample size (n=204) may be underpowered to detect smaller but clinically meaningful treatment effects.

Journal of critical care

Disparities between peripheral perfusion and sublingual and nailfold microcirculation in patients with septic shock and healthy volunteers

Romano JD, Dubin A, Mugno M et al. · 2026 Sep 22
Study Type: Cross-sectional observational study
Key Question: Does peripheral perfusion (capillary refill time [CRT], perfusion index [PI]) reliably reflect sublingual and nailfold microcirculatory status in septic shock?
Key Findings:
  • Septic shock patients (n=29) showed marked sublingual/nailfold microcirculatory derangement, prolonged CRT, and reduced PI versus healthy volunteers (n=34).
  • CRT correlated with flow-related microcirculatory parameters but not with perfused vascular density (a diffusion-related parameter); nailfold microcirculation showed no association with either CRT or PI.
  • A subset of patients with normal CRT still had abnormal sublingual microcirculation; ROC analysis showed CRT had limited diagnostic accuracy for detecting microcirculatory alteration.
Clinical Relevance: For UK intensive care clinicians relying on CRT as a simple, bedside resuscitation target, these findings caution that a normal CRT does not exclude ongoing microcirculatory dysfunction, particularly diffusion-related impairment relevant to tissue oxygenation.
Limitations: Small single-timepoint sample without longitudinal or outcome data limits generalisability and causal inference regarding resuscitation guidance.

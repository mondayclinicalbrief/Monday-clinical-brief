European Journal of Anaesthesiology

Sugammadex versus neostigmine for neuromuscular blockade reversal in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials

Putzu A, Park CH, Hu X et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs with trial sequential analysis (update of 2017 Cochrane review); 165 trials, 19,124 adults.
Key Question: In adults receiving rocuronium or vecuronium for elective surgery, how do sugammadex and neostigmine compare for efficacy and safety of NMB reversal?
Key Findings:
  • Sugammadex 2 mg/kg reversed from second twitch to TOF ratio >0.9 a mean 10.5 min faster than neostigmine 0.05 mg/kg (95% CI −11.90 to −9.08; moderate certainty).
  • Fewer patients had at least one adverse event with sugammadex (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.84; moderate certainty).
  • Sugammadex probably reduced residual NMB, inadequate reversal, desaturation, atelectasis, airway obstruction, bradycardia, dry mouth, urinary retention and increased airway secretions (moderate certainty).
Clinical Relevance: Falling sugammadex costs may strengthen the case for wider use in NHS practice, which may support local reappraisal of reversal-agent policy.
Limitations: Risk of bias was generally unclear or high, and quantitative monitoring was reported in only 113 of 165 trials, so the certainty of the findings is limited.

Anesthesiology

Individualized mean arterial pressure during cardiac surgery The OPTIPAM Randomized Clinical Trial

Descamps R, Mertes PM, Charvin M et al. · 2026 Nov 06
Study Type: Multicentre, single-blind RCT (10 tertiary centres, France); stopped early by the DSMB for safety.
Key Question: Does targeting an individualised MAP (±10% of preoperative baseline, intraoperatively and for 24 h postoperatively) improve outcomes versus standard MAP ≥65 mm Hg in elective cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass?
Key Findings:
  • Composite of death, AKI or neurological complications at 7 days: 32% (27/85) individualised vs 29% (25/87) standard (RR 1.1; 95% CI 0.7–1.8; P=0.76). Underpowered at 172 patients.
  • Hemorrhagic complications were higher with individualised targets (18% vs 3%; P=0.003), prompting early termination.
  • Mean intraoperative MAP was higher (80.6 vs 75.6 mm Hg) with greater vasopressor use, despite target adherence for only 33% of intraoperative time.
Clinical Relevance: Does not support routine adoption of baseline-derived MAP targets in UK cardiac anaesthesia, and flags a bleeding safety signal that warrants caution if higher targets are used.
Limitations: Early termination and small sample size leave the efficacy question unanswered, and poor adherence to the individualised target limits interpretation.

Anesthesiology

Project SPRUCE: Formation of an International Consortium of Pediatric Anesthesia Groups to Reduce Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Inhaled Anesthetics

Hansen EE, Devavaram P, Li G et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Multicentre quality improvement (QI) collaborative, using before-and-after comparison across 12 paediatric anaesthesia groups (no control arm described in abstract).
Key Question: Can a QI consortium using change management methods reduce greenhouse gas emissions from inhaled anaesthetics in paediatric practice?
Key Findings:
  • Inhaled anaesthetic GHG emissions fell by 63% overall (range 43–91% across sites), exceeding the 50% target.
  • Nitrous oxide use incidence fell 62.4%, with average volume per case down 83.5%.
  • Estimated 14,381 metric tonnes CO2e avoided; interventions were education/socialisation, practice constraints (e.g. agent restriction), and direct data feedback to clinicians.
Clinical Relevance: Provides a transferable, multi-site model for achieving NHS Net Zero anaesthesia targets, relevant to desflurane and nitrous oxide reduction already prioritised in UK practice.
Limitations: Uncontrolled before-and-after design and wide inter-site variation limit causal inference and generalisability; no clinical safety or outcome data are reported in the abstract.

Journal of Clinical Anesthesia

Cumulative multi-threshold intraoperative blood pressure exposure patterns and postoperative mortality

Barboi C, Boyd L, Schnetz MP et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study (single-system EHR data; decision-tree/recursive partitioning analysis)
Key Question: Does cumulative intraoperative MAP exposure across multiple thresholds (50-150 mmHg) predict 30-day mortality better than exposure below MAP 65 mmHg alone?
Key Findings:
  • Among 388,407 non-cardiac, non-obstetric surgical patients, 30-day mortality was 1.49%.
  • The multi-threshold model had higher validation AUROC than the MAP <65 model (0.619 vs 0.591; ΔAUROC 0.028, 95% CI 0.025-0.037), but discrimination remained poor for both.
  • Mortality generally rose with deeper hypotension and longer time below thresholds, though not uniformly monotonically. Medication associations were exploratory and varied between groups.
Clinical Relevance: Supports the view that pressure-time burden matters beyond a single MAP <65 cut-off, but the data do not justify changing current hypotension targets in UK practice.
Limitations: Lack of external validation, retrospective design with likely confounding, and modest discrimination (AUROC <0.65) preclude causal or practical clinical inference.

Anaesthesia

Rapid sequence induction: a narrative review

Cook TM, Plaat F, McNarry AF et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Narrative review (not a systematic review; no stated search strategy or quantitative synthesis in the abstract).
Key Question: What is the evidence base for the contested components of rapid sequence induction (RSI), and where are the gaps?
Key Findings:
  • Many RSI elements lack robust evidence or consensus. Nasogastric tubes reduce but do not reliably empty gastric contents. Gastric ultrasound's role in guiding technique is uncertain.
  • Correctly applied cricoid force obstructs the hypopharynx but has limited impact on airway management. Incorrect application can cause clinically significant difficulty. Ultrasound may be needed to locate the cricoid, and the correct force varies with age and sex. Training with feedback improves application. Airway rescue may require releasing cricoid force.
  • Videolaryngoscopy during RSI is supported by evidence. Cardiovascular complications are common, particularly in critically ill patients, and technique modifications may reduce them.
Clinical Relevance: Reinforces individualised, evidence-aware RSI practice relevant to NHS airway management, aspiration risk assessment and medicolegal defensibility, with emphasis on cricoid technique, videolaryngoscopy and haemodynamic risk.
Limitations: As a narrative review, it is vulnerable to selection bias and offers no quantitative effect estimates, so conclusions rest on authors' judgement and an acknowledged weak evidence base.

Anaesthesia

Causes of bias in Delphi studies and consensus statements: a methodological review

Nasa P, Jain R, Juneja D et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Methodological review (narrative synthesis with concept mapping); not a systematic review of clinical outcomes.
Key Question: Which cognitive biases threaten the validity of Delphi studies and consensus statements, and at which stages of the process do they arise?
Key Findings:
  • Ten cognitive biases were mapped across Delphi stages (design, panel selection, survey development, iterative scoring, consensus interpretation).
  • These were grouped into four domains: steering-group/design biases, panel composition/contextual biases, social influence/conformity biases, and individual cognitive heuristics.
  • Proposed mitigations include diverse panels, neutral survey wording, anonymous voting, methodological expertise on steering groups, and transparent iterative feedback.
Clinical Relevance: Many anaesthetic guidelines and society consensus statements rely on Delphi methodology, so clinicians can use this framework to appraise how far such recommendations should shape local practice.
Limitations: The framework is conceptual and has not been prospectively validated, and the abstract does not state how literature was identified or selected.

British Journal of Anaesthesia

Counting reactions, not just variants: what malignant hyperthermia can teach precision medicine

Riazi S, Snak de Souza CD, Ibarra Moreno CA et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Editorial/commentary on a recent BJA study (Aboelsaod et al.), which used multi-source epidemiological, genomic, contracture-test and Bayesian modelling. Not original research.
Key Question: How can malignant hyperthermia (MH) genetics move beyond binary variant classification to better estimate who will actually react to triggering agents?
Key Findings:
  • The commentary describes a threshold model: variants confer differing degrees of biological liability (modified by genetic background and environment), but once the threshold is crossed, the probability of reacting per triggering exposure appears broadly similar.
  • Integrating imperfect data sources (national epidemiology, exposure histories, population genomics, contracture testing) improved prevalence estimates for rare, exposure-dependent disease.
  • The authors argue for variant-specific penetrance estimates incorporating ancestry, genetic background, and exposure history. No numerical estimates are given in this abstract.
Clinical Relevance: Refined penetrance estimates could eventually improve counselling and risk stratification for MH-susceptible patients and relatives, including those referred to UK MH investigation services.
Limitations: Not applicable (commentary); the abstract reports no quantitative results, so the underlying study should be consulted for effect sizes.

British Journal of Anaesthesia

Cardiac output monitoring using an automatic arm cuff device in surgical patients

Jazini M, Daher H, Kumar R et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Prospective observational method-comparison study (single-centre implied; 34 patients, 191 measurement sets).
Key Question: Can a custom automatic arm cuff device estimate cardiac output (CO) as accurately as pulse contour analysis, using pulmonary artery catheter thermodilution as reference?
Key Findings:
  • Cuff-based CO estimation correlated modestly with thermodilution (r = 0.61, 95% CI 0.37–0.79); concordance (trending ability) was 83% (75–91%).
  • Pulse contour analysis of the invasive arterial waveform performed similarly (r = 0.59, 0.37–0.75; concordance 81%, 72–90%).
  • Population was liver transplant (n=24) and cardiac surgery (n=10) patients; Bland-Altman agreement and percentage error were not reported in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: A noninvasive cuff-based CO trend monitor could extend haemodynamic monitoring to patients who would not receive an arterial line, though this remains proof-of-concept.
Limitations: Small, highly selected cohort using a custom prototype, with moderate correlation and no reported percentage error, so it falls well short of accepted accuracy standards (e.g., ±30%) and is not generalisable.

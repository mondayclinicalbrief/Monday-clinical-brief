Comparative Effectiveness of Haloperidol, Droperidol, and Ondansetron for Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome in the Emergency Department

O'Brien J, Patel M, Brown J et al. · 2026 Nov
Study Type: Multicentre retrospective cohort study (TriNetX database, 2014–2024) with propensity-score matching.
Key Question: Do haloperidol or droperidol reduce short-term ED re-attendance compared with ondansetron in adults with suspected cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome?
Key Findings:
  • 72-h ED return was lower with haloperidol than ondansetron (8.2% vs 15.1%; OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.41–0.62).
  • Droperidol was similarly superior to ondansetron (7.4% vs 14.0%; OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.32–0.76); droperidol and haloperidol did not differ significantly (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.47–1.20).
  • Coded adverse events were rare across all groups.
Clinical Relevance: Supports butyrophenones over ondansetron for CHS in UK EDs, though it is hypothesis-generating only; dosing, route and QT monitoring require prospective evaluation.
Limitations: Retrospective, coded-data design with ICD-10-defined CHS and likely residual confounding (e.g., by indication), plus probable under-ascertainment of adverse events and of returns to other providers.

Annals of Emergency Medicine

Trends in Care and Outcomes Among Emergency Department Patients With Chest Pain Without Myocardial Infarction

Chou SC, Wharam JF, Hsia RY · 2026 Nov 06
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study (US commercial insurance claims data, 2007–2021)
Key Question: How have hospitalisation, downstream cardiac care, and 180-day AMI outcomes changed over time among ED patients with chest pain without an AMI diagnosis?
Key Findings:
  • Among 773,250 index visits, adjusted hospitalisation fell from 42.7% to 18.6%, 7-day noninvasive testing from 26.7% to 9.1%, and 30-day revascularisation from 5.8% to 3.6%.
  • 180-day AMI hospitalisation showed no significant overall trend (0.8–1.2%).
  • A modest upward trend in AMI hospitalisation was seen in patients aged ≥70 years.
Clinical Relevance: Supports ongoing safe-discharge and reduced-admission approaches to low-risk chest pain, though the signal in older patients may caution against uniform pathway application in this group.
Limitations: US commercially insured cohort with claims-based diagnosis, limiting generalisability to NHS populations and precluding adjustment for clinical detail such as troponin strategy; older adults are also likely under-represented in a commercial insurer population.

Long-Term Survival Following Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation: A Two-Center Five-Year Analysis

Alexy T, Beery J, Gottula AL et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Retrospective two-centre cohort study (Minnesota and Prague), 2013–2022.
Key Question: Among patients discharged alive after ECPR for refractory VT/VF OHCA, how durable are long-term survival and neurological recovery?
Key Findings:
  • Of 728 ECPR patients, 211 (29.0%) survived to discharge. Among these, survival was 76.9% at 1 year and 67.2% at 5 years, with most deaths within 90 days of discharge.
  • CPC 1–2 was 78.7% at discharge and 93.4–94.2% among survivors beyond 1 year.
  • Five-year survival fell with age (91.6% at 18–39 years vs 52.9% at >70 years); discharge disposition also stratified prognosis.
Clinical Relevance: Supports the durability of benefit from ECPR in selected patients, which is relevant as UK services develop ECPR pathways for refractory VT/VF OHCA.
Limitations: Only hospital survivors were analysed, so the findings cannot inform selection or overall ECPR effectiveness; the study was retrospective, only 32% reached 5-year follow-up, and results come from two high-volume academic centres, limiting generalisability to UK systems.

Academic Emergency Medicine

Geriatric Emergency Management Programs and Days at Home Among People Living With Dementia in Ontario

Lo M, Lee J, Bagley E et al. · 2026 Nov
Study Type: Population-based retrospective cohort study (Ontario, Canada; n=145,874)
Key Question: Is attending an ED with a Geriatric Emergency Management (GEM) program associated with more days alive and at home in older adults with dementia?
Key Findings:
  • After propensity score overlap weighting, GEM ED attendance was associated with 7.5 more days alive and at home over 12 months (95% CI 3.1–12.0); about half of this reflected fewer days in long-term care.
  • GEM ED attendance was associated with fewer subsequent ED visits.
  • Index ED length of stay and hospital admission rates were higher at GEM EDs.
Clinical Relevance: Supports the case for dedicated geriatric ED services (comparable to UK frailty and older-person ED models) as a means of delaying institutionalisation in dementia.
Limitations: Observational design with ED-level exposure means residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causality cannot be inferred. The absolute benefit (about one week per year) is modest.

Academic Emergency Medicine

Characteristics of Emergency Department Visits by Adults With Down Syndrome: A Single-Institution Retrospective Perspective

Hunt EA, Johnson KF, Lucus EJ et al. · 2026 Nov
Study Type: Single-centre retrospective chart review (descriptive, no comparator analysis)
Key Question: How do adults with Down syndrome use the emergency department, and what are their presenting complaints, investigations and dispositions?
Key Findings:
  • 177 ED visits by 106 adults (2019–2023); 69.8% attended once and 52.9% were under 40. Commonest complaints were abdominal pain (10.2%) and seizure (7.9%).
  • High resource use: laboratory testing in 82.5% and imaging in 74.6% of visits, consistent across age groups.
  • Admission rate was 38.4% (51.4% discharged home), reported as higher than local and national rates; no statistical comparison was reported in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: Adults with Down syndrome appear to have higher investigation and admission rates, which is relevant as NHS EDs see growing numbers of adults who have transitioned from paediatric care, though US findings may not map directly to UK practice.
Limitations: A single US academic centre with a small sample and descriptive design limits generalisability, and without adjustment for acuity or case-mix, the higher testing and admission rates cannot be interpreted as appropriate or excessive.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Administered opioids and benzodiazepines and the interpretation of positive trauma urine drug screens

Potts C, Ali A, Gandhi S et al. · 2026 Oct 01
Study Type: Retrospective single-centre cohort study (Level II US trauma centre, 2023–2024; 1420 of 2538 trauma patients had urine drug screens).
Key Question: How much of a positive trauma urine drug screen for opioids or benzodiazepines reflects drugs administered during care rather than pre-injury use?
Key Findings:
  • Class-matched positivity was far higher in exposed patients: fentanyl assay 70.0% vs 12.5% (aOR 15.3, 95% CI 11.5–20.4); benzodiazepines 51.6% vs 8.9% (aOR 6.1). The effect persisted for pre-hospital-only administration (fentanyl aOR 19.9). Negative-control drug classes showed no meaningful association.
  • Modelled attribution to administered drug: 62.8% (56.9–68.6) of fentanyl positives, 33.7% of opiate positives, 29.3% of benzodiazepine positives.
  • Any-positive prevalence was 64.4% overall but 37.3% in patients given neither class.
Clinical Relevance: UK trauma registry (e.g. TARN), toxicology, and safeguarding or substance-use decisions that rely on urine screens should account for pre-hospital and ED analgesia and sedation.
Limitations: Observational association from a single US centre; it cannot establish the source or analytical validity of an individual positive result.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Secure chat and critical/urgent imaging-result communication in a multisite emergency department system

Simon EL, Mann W, Baskin B et al. · 2026 Oct 24
Study Type: Prospective pre-post cohort study (operational outcomes) with concurrent survey; 26 EDs in a US academic health system.
Key Question: Does an EHR-integrated Secure Chat-first protocol (with verbal failsafe) for critical/urgent imaging results maintain safe closed-loop communication without impairing radiologist workflow?
Key Findings:
  • Documented critical/urgent result communications rose 26.5% (mean +21.4 per radiologist; 95% CI 2.9–39.8; p=0.026).
  • Radiologist interpretation throughput was unchanged (+0.58%; p=0.833), with no association with Secure Chat volume (R²=0.023).
  • No Secure Chat-associated safety events were found in 1,087 reviewed communications. Most emergency physicians (77%) and radiologists (95%) supported it as a supplementary modality.
Clinical Relevance: Offers early evidence that EHR-integrated messaging could supplement telephone-based critical result reporting, relevant to NHS departments considering digital alternatives to verbal-only escalation.
Limitations: Uncontrolled pre-post design with a pilot limited to overnight shifts, and low emergency physician survey response (20.2%), risking selection bias and limiting causal inference and generalisability to NHS settings.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Tachycardia and rhabdomyolysis after use of melanotan and an uncommon synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonist

Mullins ME, Pentecost G, Li JL · 2026 Oct 25
Study Type: Single case report (no cohort, comparator or quantitative data in the abstract).
Key Question: What was the cause of tachycardia, tremor and rhabdomyolysis in a young man presenting after melanotan injection?
Key Findings:
  • A young adult male presented with tachycardia and tremor after reported melanotan (synthetic α-MSH agonist, used for tanning) injection, with rhabdomyolysis per the title.
  • Laboratory testing identified an undisclosed, rarely reported synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonist (SCRA), which the patient had not declared.
  • The authors discuss likely contributions from both substances; the abstract gives no vital signs, CK values or outcome.
Clinical Relevance: Internet-sourced "cosmetic" injectables may be contaminated or co-used with novel psychoactive substances, so consider toxicology confirmation and CK measurement in unexplained tachycardia and tremor, particularly given the UK's illicit melanotan market and standard immunoassays missing novel SCRAs.
Limitations: A single case cannot establish causation or attribute effects to either agent, and the abstract provides no quantitative detail.

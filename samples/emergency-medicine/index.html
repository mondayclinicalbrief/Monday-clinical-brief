Racial and Demographic Differences in Use of a Trauma Center Confidential Status Policy

Wodecki DJ, Osowiecki I, Adams C et al. · 2026 Jul
Study Type: Mixed-methods study (retrospective cohort analysis plus survey/qualitative interviews), single-centre.
Key Question: Are there racial/demographic disparities in application of a trauma centre "confidential status" policy, and how do staff perceive its effects on care?
Key Findings:
  • Of 42,540 trauma patients (2015–2020), 319 were placed on confidential status; 62% had assault-related injuries.
  • Black patients had significantly higher odds of confidential status placement than White patients (OR 2.69, 95% CI 1.97–3.68); Latine patients also had higher odds (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.03–2.07).
  • Only 38% of staff had read the policy; most reported neutral or negative perceptions, with qualitative themes of family trauma, discrimination, workplace stress, and policy ineffectiveness.
Clinical Relevance: UK EDs implementing safeguarding/confidentiality protocols for violence-injured patients should audit for equity impacts, as similar undisclosed racial bias could inadvertently affect family communication and patient experience.
Limitations: Single-centre US design limits generalisability, and staff survey/interview samples were likely small and subject to selection bias.

Academic emergency medicine : official journal of the Society for Academic Emergency Medicine

Beyond a Diagnosis: A Qualitative Study Exploring Patient and Caregiver Expectations About Emergency Department Visits Amid Uncertainty

Chien LJ, Morris J, Lawless A et al. · 2026 Jul
Study Type: Qualitative study (semi-structured interviews, content and thematic analysis)
Key Question: What do patients and caregivers presenting to the ED with nonspecific symptoms expect regarding diagnosis, particularly when diagnostic uncertainty is likely?
Key Findings:
  • Expectations clustered into three themes: outcomes (understanding, treatment, guidance), processes (investigations, feeling taken seriously), and health service factors (timeliness, integrated care).
  • Most participants wanted a diagnosis, but many showed awareness and tolerance of diagnostic uncertainty, reflecting a spectrum rather than a binary expectation.
  • Patients with prior experiences of symptom invalidation had heightened expectations of being believed and taken seriously; investigations were valued even when unlikely to resolve uncertainty.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights the need for UK ED clinicians to explicitly elicit and address patient expectations and validate symptoms, supporting shared, uncertainty-tolerant diagnostic communication consistent with NHS patient-centred care priorities.
Limitations: Small sample from two Australian EDs limits generalisability to UK ED populations and systems.

A Team Approach to Patient-Centered Communication in the Emergency Department

Gutman CK, Keebler JR, Fernandez R et al. · 2026 Jul
Study Type: Commentary/conceptual paper (theoretical framework proposal, not original research)
Key Question: How can patient-centred communication (PCC) be reconceptualised to fit the time-pressured, team-based nature of ED care rather than traditional one-to-one clinician–patient models?
Key Findings:
  • Proposes viewing the ED care team (physicians, nurses, ancillary staff, patient/caregivers) as a "swift-starting action team" that must achieve PCC goals rapidly despite no prior working relationship.
  • Applies teamwork science frameworks (input–process–outcome–input model, team cognition) to reframe PCC as a distributed, coordinated process rather than an individual clinician skill.
  • No empirical data presented; this is a theoretical model intended to guide future research, training, and quality improvement.
Clinical Relevance: Offers UK EM clinicians and educators a novel framework for designing team-based communication training and interventions suited to NHS ED workflows, where communication continuity is inherently fragmented.
Limitations: As a conceptual paper, it lacks empirical validation or testing of the proposed model in real ED settings.

Emergency medicine journal : EMJ

Diagnostic accuracy of point-of-care high-sensitivity troponin algorithms for emergency department rule-out and rule-in of acute myocardial infarction: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Capodifoglio S, De Iuliis V · 2026 Jul 21
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis (8 studies, 19 datasets, 9384 patients)
Key Question: How accurate are point-of-care high-sensitivity troponin (hs-POC) serial algorithms for ruling out or ruling in AMI in the ED?
Key Findings:
  • Rule-out algorithms: pooled sensitivity 98.0% (95% CI 96.0–99.0%), NPV 99.9% at 10% prevalence; no additional missed AMIs on follow-up.
  • Rule-in algorithms: pooled specificity 96.0% (94.0–97.0%), PPV 68.5% at 10% prevalence.
  • Multiparametric algorithms outperformed simple cut-offs (sensitivity 99.3% vs 96.9%); 0h/1h protocols matched 0h/2h accuracy with faster turnaround (8–17 min); all four platforms achieved rule-out sensitivity ≥97%.
Clinical Relevance: Supports adoption of rapid hs-POC troponin pathways (0h/1h) in UK EDs to accelerate safe discharge and reduce overcrowding without compromising diagnostic safety, relevant to NHS 4-hour target pressures.
Limitations: Small number of studies per platform and heterogeneous designs limit generalisability, and outcome data on real-world workflow/patient-centred impact are lacking.

Emergency medicine journal : EMJ

Association between the timing of prehospital epinephrine administration and patient outcomes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in Korea: a nationwide observational study

Lee SH, Hong WP, Park JH et al. · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study (nationwide, propensity-score matched)
Key Question: Does earlier prehospital epinephrine administration (≤20 min vs >20 min from arrest) improve survival and neurological outcomes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest?
Key Findings:
  • In matched analysis (n=5,734), early epinephrine (≤20 min) was associated with higher survival to discharge (7.74% vs 4.46%; aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.44–0.71 for delay) and better favourable neurological outcome (CPC 1-2: 3.77% vs 2.13%; aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.40–0.79).
  • Each additional minute of delay reduced odds of good neurological outcome (aOR 0.964 per minute; aOR 0.833 per 5 minutes), with a steep decline beyond 30 minutes (aOR 0.048, 95% CI 0.006–0.393).
Clinical Relevance: Supports UK ambulance service protocols prioritising rapid IV/IO access and early epinephrine delivery within current ALS cardiac arrest algorithms, reinforcing time-sensitive care bundles in OHCA.
Limitations: Retrospective, registry-based design cannot exclude residual confounding (e.g., resuscitation quality, bystander CPR) despite propensity matching.

Emergency medicine journal : EMJ

Alcohol-related minor injuries in an emergency room setting (ARMIERo): a case-crossover study on alcohol consumption and alcohol attributable fraction of minor injuries

Paris T, Carron PN, Gmel G et al. · 2026 Jul 24
Study Type: Prospective case-crossover study (single-centre, Switzerland)
Key Question: Is acute alcohol consumption associated with increased risk of minor injury presentations to the ED, and what proportion of such injuries is attributable to alcohol?
Key Findings:
  • 18.1% of 353 participants reported acute alcohol use within 6 hours of injury; this was associated with significantly increased odds of minor injury (OR 2.65, 95% CI 1.67–4.18).
  • Alcohol-attributable fraction for minor injuries was 23%.
  • Subgroup analysis showed higher risk among chronic binge drinkers (OR 3.81, 95% CI 1.82–7.99) and women (OR 5.52, 95% CI 2.02–15.08).
Clinical Relevance: Given the high volume of minor injury attendances in UK EDs, these findings support opportunistic alcohol screening and brief intervention as a feasible strategy to reduce preventable injury burden, aligning with NHS alcohol care team initiatives.
Limitations: Single-centre design with self-reported alcohol consumption and a relatively small sample limit generalisability and risk recall/reporting bias.

Emergency medicine journal : EMJ

Non-communicable disease prevalence among asylum seekers and Swiss nationals: a comparative cross-sectional study in emergency care

Jachmann A, von Gradowski AS, Kamenshchikova A et al. · 2026 Jul 28
Study Type: Retrospective cross-sectional comparative study (matched cohort, single-centre ED, Switzerland)
Key Findings:
  • Overall NCD prevalence (34.5% vs 36.9%, p=0.475) and multimorbidity (10.2% vs 9.0%, p=0.322) were similar between asylum seekers (AS) and Swiss nationals (SN); cardiovascular disease was most common in both.
  • Diabetes prevalence was significantly higher in AS (9.3% vs 3.8%, p=0.001), particularly among women, while neoplasms and respiratory disease were less frequent.
  • ED consultation frequency and resource use were comparable (~10% NCD-related visits in each group), but untreated cardiovascular disease and diabetes were more common among AS, suggesting gaps in chronic disease management.
Clinical Relevance: UK EDs, similarly serving as default access points for asylum seekers with limited primary care access, should recognise potential undertreated chronic disease burden and consider opportunistic screening/referral pathways for NCDs in this population.
Limitations: Single-centre design using routine electronic records limits generalisability and may underestimate true NCD prevalence due to reliance on documented diagnoses.

A socioeconomic equity measured by Indices of Multiple Deprivation for HEMS-attended major trauma in South East England: heterogenous effects in severe traumatic brain injury

Griggs J, Harris J, Barrett J et al. · 2026 Jul 28
Study Type: Retrospective cohort service evaluation
Key Question: Does socioeconomic deprivation influence 30-day mortality and equity of care delivery in HEMS-attended major trauma patients in South East England?
Key Findings:
  • No socioeconomic gradient in 30-day mortality (17% Q1 vs 21% Q5, p=0.861; adjusted OR per quintile 1.01, 95% CI 0.92–1.11)
  • Equitable delivery of key interventions across deprivation quintiles: prehospital emergency anaesthesia (37–43%), blood transfusion (8–13%), night operations (28–41%), all non-significant
  • Significant GCS×deprivation interaction in traumatic brain injury subgroup (p=0.003), suggesting possible differential effects by injury severity/age not seen overall
Clinical Relevance: Supports the equity value of regional physician-paramedic HEMS models in mitigating socioeconomic disparities in major trauma outcomes, relevant to NHS trauma network commissioning and resource allocation.
Limitations: Deprivation was assigned by incident location rather than patient residence, potentially misclassifying true socioeconomic exposure, and TBI subgroup findings are exploratory/hypothesis-generating.

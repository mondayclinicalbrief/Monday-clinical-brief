Frailty and Hospitalization After Emergency Department Evaluation Within Triage Categories in Older Patients: A Retrospective Cohort Study

Hata N, Nagae M, Umegaki H · 2026 Oct
Study Type: Single-centre retrospective cohort study (Japanese tertiary hospital; 3127 ED visits, patients ≥65 years).
Key Question: Is frailty (CFS ≥5) associated with hospitalisation after ED evaluation within triage urgency categories, and does this vary by category?
Key Findings:
  • 1526/3127 visits (49%) resulted in admission or acute-care transfer.
  • Adjusted ORs for frailty: JTAS 2 1.00 (95% CI 0.65–1.55); JTAS 3 1.54 (1.19–1.99); JTAS 4/5 2.01 (1.13–3.58). JTAS 1 could not be estimated (quasi-complete separation).
  • Global frailty-by-triage interaction was non-significant (p=0.091), so variation across categories is unproven.
Clinical Relevance: Frailty appears to influence admission decisions beyond triage urgency in lower-acuity older patients, which is relevant to NHS discussions on frailty-informed pathways and same-day emergency care.
Limitations: Retrospective single-centre design with complete-case analysis and Japanese-specific triage and admission practices limits generalisability to UK settings.

Academic Emergency Medicine

Differentiating Normal Healing From Infection in Total Joint Arthroplasty: An Educational and Diagnostic Tool for Emergency Providers Evaluating the Postoperative Patient

Brown TW, Carusi AT, Langworthy M · 2026 Sep
Study Type: Narrative review with proposed diagnostic algorithm (not a systematic review; no search methodology or pooled data reported in abstract).
Key Question: How can emergency clinicians distinguish normal postoperative inflammation from superficial surgical site infection or early periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) within 30 days of total joint arthroplasty?
Key Findings:
  • Normal early healing produces inflammatory marker and synovial fluid changes that often exceed thresholds validated for chronic PJI, so standard cut-offs risk false positives.
  • Early PJI diagnosis needs time-dependent interpretation of synovial fluid, whereas superficial SSI remains a clinical diagnosis.
  • The authors propose a structured algorithm intended to reduce unnecessary testing and cost; no diagnostic accuracy or outcome data are given in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: Useful for UK EDs seeing early post-arthroplasty presentations, where chronic-PJI thresholds could prompt unnecessary aspiration or referral, though the framework is US-oriented (e.g. TEAM bundled payments) and needs adapting to local orthopaedic pathways.
Limitations: The algorithm appears to be an expert synthesis without prospective validation, and the abstract does not report its sensitivity, specificity, or safety outcomes.

Academic Emergency Medicine

Smartphone-Based Measurement of Cognition and Physical Function in Older Emergency Department Patients: A Feasibility Study

Hartman CA, Leroux A, Merchant RC et al. · 2026 Sep
Study Type: Prospective single-cohort feasibility study (US ED setting).
Key Question: Is smartphone-based assessment of cognition and physical function feasible in older adults attending the ED after a fall?
Key Findings:
  • Of 196 patients (mean age 78.2, 68% women), 77% attempted at least one digital assessment. Among those attempting, cognitive test completion was 68-87%, and was associated with absence of cognitive impairment (p ≤ 0.01).
  • Physical function test completion was only 18-20%, mainly due to safety concerns, pain and injury; completion was associated with better Barthel Index scores and non-use of mobility aids.
  • Common barriers to cognitive testing were lack of glasses, feeling unwell and technology problems.
Clinical Relevance: Brief digital cognitive screening may be deliverable in busy UK ED falls pathways, but in-department digital physical assessment appears impractical.
Limitations: Feasibility only, with no validation against clinical outcomes, and the US single-setting sample may limit applicability to NHS EDs.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Acute kidney injury secondary to Djenkol bean ingestion

Audette M, Mankoff Z, Heintz H et al. · 2026 Sep 13
Study Type: Single case report
Key Question: What is the presentation and management of acute kidney injury following djenkol bean ingestion in a non-endemic setting?
Key Findings:
  • A 10-year-old boy presented with abdominal pain, frank haematuria and AKI the day after eating djenkol beans; other causes of AKI were excluded (no specific creatinine or urinalysis values given in the abstract).
  • Treated with IV bicarbonate infusion and overnight admission; renal function improved, haematuria resolved, and he was discharged.
  • Reported as the first US case of djenkolism.
Clinical Relevance: Djenkolism is a rare differential for unexplained AKI with haematuria and abdominal pain in patients with Southeast Asian dietary exposure, so a food history is worth taking in diverse UK populations.
Limitations: A single case without quantified renal indices or follow-up cannot establish treatment efficacy; recovery may have occurred spontaneously.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Dual direct current external cardioversion in refractory atrial fibrillation: a systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis

Ayyad M, Ayyad A, SayyedAhmad D et al. · 2026 Sep 16
Study Type: Systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis (7 studies pooled; 8 in qualitative synthesis), predominantly observational.
Key Question: How effective and safe is dual simultaneous external DC cardioversion in adults with refractory AF after failed single-defibrillator cardioversion?
Key Findings:
  • Pooled acute cardioversion success was 79% (95% CI 74-83%; I² = 0%).
  • Ventricular arrhythmias occurred in 1% (95% CI 0-4%); transient bradyarrhythmia in 13% (95% CI 4-31%); no deaths reported.
  • Exploratory subgroups showed no significant difference in success by pad configuration or number of prior failed single-defibrillator attempts.
Clinical Relevance: This is a possible rescue option for ED patients with AF refractory to standard cardioversion (particularly those with high transthoracic impedance), but it is not yet supported for routine UK practice.
Limitations: Without a comparator arm and with heterogeneous procedures and few contemporary biphasic data, the review cannot establish comparative efficacy, safety, or optimal patient selection.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Serum hyperkalemia and short-term clinical outcomes in emergency department patients: A multicenter propensity-matched study

O'Brien J, Patel MJ, Schrock JW · 2026 Sep 16
Study Type: Multicentre retrospective cohort study (TriNetX database, US EDs, 2010–2024), propensity-matched.
Key Question: Is there a graded association between initial serum potassium and 48-hour adverse outcomes in non-dialysis ED patients?
Key Findings:
  • Composite of cardiac arrest, VT or VF within 48 h rose from 3.05% (K⁺ 5.5–5.99) to 6.68% (≥7.0 mmol/L), versus 0.77–0.90% in matched normokalaemic controls (RR 3.97–8.08).
  • 48-hour mortality rose from 2.04% to 5.79% across strata, with similarly higher ICU admission and ventilation rates.
  • At K⁺ 5.01–5.49, composite risk was 1.52% vs 0.85% (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.75–1.83), but isolated VT/VF was not increased.
Clinical Relevance: Supports using initial potassium as a risk-stratification marker in UK EDs, though it does not guide treatment thresholds.
Limitations: Retrospective design with residual confounding likely; the authors state causation and dose-response are not established.

American Journal of Emergency Medicine

Contrast agent substitution or corticosteroid premedication: association with emergency department throughput

Sertic M, Johnston H, Dempsey PJ et al. · 2026 Sep 16
Study Type: Retrospective comparative study across two US academic emergency departments (790 patients).
Key Question: Does substituting the contrast agent, rather than giving corticosteroid premedication, reduce ED time-to-CT and time-to-discharge in patients with a prior mild allergic-like reaction to iodinated contrast?
Key Findings:
  • Time from CT order to scan was 4.28 h shorter with agent substitution (n=63) than with steroid premedication (n=727) (95% CI 3.85-4.72 h).
  • Time to ED discharge was 3.97 h shorter (95% CI 2.72-5.30 h).
  • No breakthrough reactions occurred in the substitution group (95% CI 0-5.7%).
Clinical Relevance: Standard 5-13 h premedication regimens substantially delay imaging and prolong ED stay, which matters for NHS flow and 4-hour performance, though substitution requires knowing the culprit agent.
Limitations: Non-randomised, with allocation determined by whether the culprit agent was known (confounding), and the small substitution group gives a wide upper confidence limit for breakthrough reactions; the study was not designed to compare safety.

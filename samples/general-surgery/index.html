British Journal of Surgery

Small-bite compared with large-bite closure of midline laparotomy: meta-analysis of short- and long-term outcomes with trial sequential analysis

Emile SH, Elhadad A, Elsayed AH · 2026 Oct 05
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs with trial sequential analysis (10 RCTs, 2617 patients).
Key Question: Does small-bite (5 mm) fascial closure of midline laparotomy reduce incisional hernia and other complications compared with large-bite (10 mm) closure?
Key Findings:
  • Incisional hernia was reduced with small-bite closure: RR 0.57 (95% CI 0.39-0.82) in studies using standardised clinical plus imaging assessment; RR 0.48 (0.33-0.70) in studies with >12 months' follow-up; RR 0.56 (0.36-0.89) with ≥24 months' follow-up. TSA showed firm evidence of benefit despite not reaching the required information size.
  • Small-bite closure also reduced SSI (RR 0.72) and total complications (RR 0.76), with no difference in seroma, fascial dehiscence or pain.
  • Closure time was longer by a mean of 4.82 minutes.
Clinical Relevance: Findings support adopting small-bite closure as standard for elective and emergency midline laparotomy in the NHS, at minimal time cost and without additional equipment.
Limitations: Inconsistent hernia ascertainment across trials (clinical-only assessment gave larger effects, likely inflating benefit), and TSA did not reach the required information size.

British Journal of Surgery

Long-term oncological outcomes of robotic versus laparoscopic total mesorectal excision for rectal cancer: COLRAR randomized clinical trial

Song SH, Min BS, Park JS et al. · 2026 Oct 05
Study Type: Randomised controlled trial (5-year follow-up of the COLRAR trial; three Korean tertiary centres, modified intention-to-treat).
Key Question: Does robotic TME give equivalent 5-year oncological outcomes to laparoscopic TME in rectal cancer within 10 cm of the anal verge?
Key Findings:
  • No significant differences in 5-year overall survival (92.4% robotic vs 92.6% laparoscopic; P=0.838) or relapse-free survival (83.0% vs 81.9%; P=0.801).
  • Local recurrence was 1.3% (robotic) vs 4.9% (laparoscopic), but 5-year local recurrence-free survival did not differ significantly (P=0.076); distant recurrence-free survival was also similar (P=0.655).
  • Post-hoc subgroup (n=146, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy): 5-year local recurrence-free survival 98.6% robotic vs 90.7% laparoscopic (P=0.039).
Clinical Relevance: Supports robotic TME as oncologically non-inferior to laparoscopic TME, which is relevant to UK units expanding robotic rectal cancer programmes; the chemoradiotherapy-subgroup signal is hypothesis-generating only.
Limitations: Long-term outcomes were pre-specified secondary endpoints, and the study was likely underpowered for these, so the subgroup finding is post-hoc, unadjusted for multiplicity, and cannot be considered confirmatory.

British Journal of Surgery

Efficacy of indocyanine green (ICG) angiography in reducing postoperative complications in oesophagectomy: systematic review and meta-analysis

Dermanis AA, Thompson D, Kamarajah SK et al. · 2026 Oct 05
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis (23 studies, including 4 RCTs; 2,845 patients, 1,154 with ICG assessment)
Key Question: Does intraoperative ICG fluorescence angiography reduce anastomotic leakage and other postoperative complications after oesophagectomy?
Key Findings:
  • ICG use was associated with lower anastomotic leakage (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.29–0.65; NNT 13) and conduit necrosis (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.85).
  • No significant differences in mortality (OR 1.43, 95% CI 0.69–2.97), reoperation (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.51–1.39), severe leak, overall morbidity, readmission, operating time, or length of stay.
  • GRADE certainty was low or very low for all outcomes.
Clinical Relevance: ICG is a low-cost adjunct that may reduce leak rates in UK oesophagectomy practice, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to mandate routine use or to justify claims of improved survival or reduced reoperation.
Limitations: Most included studies were observational, and ICG assessment remains qualitative and operator-dependent, so confounding and heterogeneity limit confidence in the effect estimates.

Annals of Surgery

Multimodal Prediction in Robotic Surgery: The Incremental Value of Task-Level Kinematic Data

Ballo M, Billato I, Godbole AA et al. · 2026 Oct 22
Study Type: Retrospective single-dataset predictive modelling study (machine learning, cross-validated); no external validation reported.
Key Question: Do task-level kinematic performance indicators add predictive value beyond preoperative clinical variables for surgeon experience, case complexity, and outcomes in robotic cholecystectomy?
Key Findings:
  • Kinematic indicators alone matched combined models for surgeon experience (AUROC 0.967 vs 0.964) and case complexity (0.791 vs 0.792), and outperformed clinical variables alone.
  • For outcomes, the combined model performed best but gains were modest (AUROC 0.753 vs 0.720 for either single modality; P=0.015 and 0.005).
  • Kinematic features contributed 90.7%, 73.3% and 51.7% of SHAP value for experience, complexity and outcomes respectively.
Clinical Relevance: Supports the potential of automated robotic performance metrics for objective trainee assessment, though outcome prediction remains too weak for clinical use as the NHS expands robotic cholecystectomy and training.
Limitations: Small, single-source retrospective sample (234 dissections, only 36 by trainees) without external validation, and the outcome endpoint is not defined in the abstract.

Annals of Surgery

Endoscopic Classification of Fundoplication Integrity Predicts Pathologic Reflux: Development and Prospective Validation

Siboni S, De Maron R, Lovece A et al. · 2026 Oct 29
Study Type: Single-centre, two-phase cohort study (retrospective development, prospective validation).
Key Question: Can a simple endoscopic classification of fundoplication integrity predict pathologic reflux after antireflux surgery?
Key Findings:
  • Three retroflexed-view parameters correlated with pathologic GERD (Lyon 2.0): intrathoracic valve, disrupted valve, and visible hiatus.
  • Pathologic GERD rose stepwise with the number of impaired mechanisms (0 to 3): 11.8%, 37.5%, 85.7%. Symptoms, manometric features, and reflux burden worsened in parallel.
  • AUC for discriminating pathologic GERD was 0.860 (development, n=73) and 0.870 (validation, n=49).
Clinical Relevance: Endoscopy is already routine in assessing post-fundoplication symptoms, so a simple three-item score could help triage which patients need physiology testing or revisional surgery assessment, particularly where access to HRM and pH monitoring is limited.
Limitations: Small single-centre cohort (n=122) with a modest validation set and no reported inter-observer agreement, limiting generalisability to routine practice.

Annals of Surgery

Out in the Match: Navigating Identity and Psychological Safety for LGBTQ+ General Surgery Applicants

Xie S, Bradley SE, Picart JK et al. · 2026 Oct 29
Study Type: Qualitative study (semistructured interviews, n=17, purposive and snowball sampling; US)
Key Question: How do LGBTQ+ general surgery residents navigate identity disclosure and assess psychological safety when applying through the residency Match?
Key Findings:
  • Applicants disclosed identity strategically, either as a "litmus test" to filter for affirming programmes or concealed it, fearing it would reduce their chance of being ranked.
  • Psychological safety was judged from subtle cues (pronouns, pride symbols), the tone and manner of responses to LGBTQ+-related questions, and visible LGBTQ+ resident/faculty representation.
  • Applicants used geography and regional sociopolitical climate as a proxy for acceptance.
Clinical Relevance: Although set in the US Match, the findings may inform inclusive recruitment and selection practice for UK surgical training and consultant-led interview panels, where EDI and workforce retention are priorities.
Limitations: Small, predominantly White sample of current residents (selection and survivor bias), with US-specific context limiting transferability to UK training pathways.

Annals of Surgery

Surgeon Perspectives on Pregnancy Loss

Siderides C, Cain-Trivette CJ, Harris H et al. · 2026 Oct 30
Study Type: Qualitative study (semistructured focus groups and individual interviews, thematic analysis). Sample size not reported in the abstract.
Key Question: What are female general surgery trainees' and recent graduates' experiences of pregnancy loss during training, and what gaps exist in programme support?
Key Findings:
  • Pregnancy loss had pervasive mental health, physical and professional impacts on participants.
  • Five barriers to seeking support were identified: burden to colleagues, privacy concerns, reputation concerns, guilt and isolation, and training rigidity. Facilitators were fewer but described as highly beneficial.
  • Themes informed proposed components of a pregnancy loss policy aimed at reducing stigma, supporting well-being and limiting attrition.
Clinical Relevance: UK training programmes and NHS trusts face similar workforce retention pressures, so these findings could inform deanery and departmental policy on compassionate leave, flexibility and confidential support for surgical trainees.
Limitations: Participants were self-selected via social media and all US-based surgeons (apparently), limiting transferability, including to the UK training context; the abstract does not report sample size.

Annals of Surgery

Hospital-level Variation in Medical Debt After Acute Traumatic Injury

Mullens CL, Becker NV, Oliphant BW et al. · 2026 Oct 30
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study (trauma registry linked to consumer credit reports; 29 US Level I/II trauma centres, 20,613 patients)
Key Question: Does the treating hospital independently influence the medical debt patients accrue after acute traumatic injury?
Key Findings:
  • Mean change in medical debt in collections was $314 (SD $3,464) from baseline to months 9-18 post-injury.
  • After risk adjustment, 4 hospitals (14%) were high outliers (+$365 to +$566 per patient versus expected) and 4 (14%) were low outliers (−$292 to −$676).
  • Low-outlier hospitals treated younger, lower-income, higher-risk patients, and results were robust to sensitivity analyses.
Clinical Relevance: Although set in the US insurance-based system and not directly transferable to the NHS, it shows that institutional administrative practices can drive patient financial harm independently of clinical factors, which is relevant to UK discussions of indirect costs of injury (lost income, out-of-pocket expenses).
Limitations: Debt in collections is a crude proxy for financial hardship, and the cohort (mean age 63, 82% non-Hispanic White, single state) limits generalisability; the specific hospital practices driving variation were not identified.

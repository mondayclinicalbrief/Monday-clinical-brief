Renal outcomes in people with type 2 diabetes with and without albuminuria treated with SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists: target trial emulation

Turchin A, Petito LC, Hegermiller E et al. · 2026 Sep 16
Study Type: Target trial emulation (observational cohort study using linked EHR/claims data)
Key Question: Do GLP-1 receptor agonists or SGLT-2 inhibitors reduce renal deterioration risk compared with DPP-4 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes, and does this benefit differ by albuminuria status?
Key Findings:
  • In people with albuminuria, 5-year risk of renal deterioration (creatinine doubling or eGFR <15) was lower with GLP-1RA/SGLT-2i vs DPP-4i (3.2% vs 5.4%; risk ratio 0.60, 95% CI 0.42–0.82).
  • In people without albuminuria, no protective effect was seen (2.4% vs 2.1%; risk ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.90–1.38).
  • Cohort of 75,455 patients, median follow-up 32 months; 82% had no albuminuria at baseline.
Clinical Relevance: Supports prioritising SGLT-2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists as second-line agents specifically in diabetic patients with albuminuria to prevent renal deterioration, informing NHS prescribing pathways and NICE-aligned risk stratification.
Limitations: Observational design with residual confounding despite target trial emulation methodology, and relatively short follow-up limits assessment of long-term renal outcomes in the non-albuminuric group.

BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

Contemporary menopausal hormone therapy and thrombotic disease: nationwide nested case-control study

Berggreen J, Pourhadi N, Wood-Kurland H et al. · 2026 Sep 23
Study Type: Nationwide nested case-control study (Danish registry data, 2003–2021)
Key Question: Does current use of menopausal hormone therapy (oral vs transdermal) increase risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), ischaemic stroke, or myocardial infarction (MI) in women aged 50–69?
Key Findings:
  • Oral oestrogen therapy (alone or combined) was associated with increased risk of VTE (HR 1.6, 95% CI 1.5–1.8), ischaemic stroke (HR 1.3, 1.2–1.4), and MI (HR 1.2, 1.1–1.3); numbers needed to harm for one year of use were 1055, 1642, and 3846 respectively.
  • High-dose oral oestradiol (>1mg/day) for >5 years carried further elevated stroke (HR 1.8) and MI (HR 1.8) risk.
  • Transdermal therapy showed no increased thrombotic risk overall, except transdermal combined cyclic therapy and MI (HR 2.1, 1.1–4.1).
Clinical Relevance: Supports UK/NHS prescribing practice favouring transdermal over oral HRT formulations to minimise thrombotic risk, particularly relevant for acute physicians assessing VTE, stroke, or MI in women on hormone therapy.
Limitations: Observational design with potential residual confounding (e.g., indication bias, unmeasured lifestyle/cardiovascular risk factors) despite matching and exclusion criteria.

BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

QTc changes and early major adverse cardiovascular events associated with antidepressant treatment for major depressive disorder in adults: individual participant data network meta-regression of double blind randomised trials

Ostinelli EG, Capocci S, Li Z et al. · 2026 Sep 23
Study Type: Individual participant data (IPD) network meta-regression combined with aggregate pairwise meta-analysis of double-blind RCTs.
Key Question: Do specific antidepressants differ in their effect on QTc interval and early cardiovascular risk during the first eight weeks of treatment for major depressive disorder?
Key Findings:
  • Escitalopram (+8.7ms, 95% CrI 1.6–15.8) and amitriptyline (+5.3ms, 0.8–9.8) significantly prolonged QTc versus placebo; when risk factors were incorporated, amitriptyline and escitalopram showed the greatest prolongation, venlafaxine and vortioxetine the least.
  • Considerable individual variability in QTc response, particularly with escitalopram, fluoxetine, and mirtazapine.
  • No association between antidepressant use and early MACE or non-suicidal sudden death (risk difference 0.01%, 95% CrI −0.01% to 0.02%; 139 trials, 52,398 participants).
Clinical Relevance: For NHS clinicians initiating antidepressants in acute/general medical settings, drug choice should account for baseline QTc and modifiable risk factors (electrolytes, renal function, BMI), though absolute cardiovascular event risk in early treatment appears low.
Limitations: Only 27% of eligible trials contributed individual-level QTc data, limiting precision and generalisability of risk-factor-adjusted estimates.

BMJ (Clinical research ed.)

When I use a word . . . Alumni of the Edinburgh Medical School 1726-2026: Erasmus Darwin versus William Withering

Aronson JK · 2026 Sep 25
Study Type: Historical/biographical commentary (medical history essay)
Key Question: What was the nature and origin of the professional rivalry between Erasmus Darwin and William Withering, two 18th-century Edinburgh-trained physicians central to the early history of digitalis therapy?
Key Findings:
  • A 1780 case in Staffordshire, where Withering's digitalis apparently succeeded after Darwin's treatment failed, was published by Darwin anonymously without crediting Withering, seemingly fuelling lasting animosity.
  • Withering countered by criticising Darwin in his seminal 1785 monograph *An Account of the Foxglove*.
  • A separate 1788 dispute over a patient's care (via Darwin's son Robert) reignited hostilities, possibly instigated or encouraged by Erasmus.
Clinical Relevance: Illustrates the historical and interpersonal origins of foundational cardiovascular pharmacology (digitalis/foxglove), of ongoing relevance to clinicians using cardiac glycosides today.
Limitations: Relies on historical correspondence and secondary interpretation rather than verifiable primary clinical data.

European journal of internal medicine

Hypertonic saline and high-dose furosemide in very elderly patients hospitalized for acute heart failure: A real-world cohort study

Ropero-Luis G, Carmona-Moreno ME, Melgar-Velasco M et al. · 2026 Sep 21
Study Type: Retrospective, single-centre, uncontrolled observational cohort study
Key Question: Is hypertonic saline plus high-dose IV furosemide feasible and effective for decongestion in very elderly, comorbid patients hospitalised with acute heart failure?
Key Findings:
  • Among 191 treatment episodes (140 patients, median age 84, 75% preserved ejection fraction, 41% advanced CKD), diuretic response was good in 41%, intermediate in 45%, poor in 13%; renal function remained stable and NT-proBNP fell.
  • Poor diuretic response strongly predicted early clinical failure (penalised OR 7.90, 95% CI 1.09–102) and markedly shorter survival (restricted mean survival 4.4 vs 27.3 days).
  • Overall 30-day mortality was high (35.7%), reflecting the frailty of this population; most episodes ended due to clinical improvement (71.2%).
Clinical Relevance: Supports feasibility and safety of aggressive combined diuretic strategies for elderly, multimorbid AHF patients typical of UK acute medicine/internal medicine wards, while highlighting poor diuretic response as an early prognostic red flag warranting escalation or goals-of-care discussion.
Limitations: Uncontrolled, single-centre design without a comparator arm limits causal inference regarding efficacy versus natural disease course.

European journal of internal medicine

Central embolic localization in acute pulmonary embolism: Prognostic marker or target for intervention?

Vedovati MC, Cimini LA, Enea I et al. · 2026 Sep 21
Study Type: Cohort study (nationwide Italian multicentre registry, COPE)
Key Question: Does central embolic localization on CTPA independently predict adverse outcomes in acute symptomatic PE, beyond established risk stratification?
Key Findings:
  • Central PE (61.5% of 4,415 patients) was not associated with all-cause 30-day death, but independently predicted PE-related death (1.7% vs 0.6%, HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.08–4.11) and death/clinical deterioration (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.05–1.79).
  • Among ESC intermediate-risk patients, central localization identified a subgroup with markedly higher PE-related mortality (1.6% vs 0.4%, HR 4.69, 95% CI 1.67–13.22).
  • Central emboli were present in >83% of high and intermediate-high risk patients, reflecting correlation with haemodynamic burden.
Clinical Relevance: For UK acute medicine clinicians, CTPA-defined central clot burden could refine risk stratification within intermediate-risk PE—a group where escalation decisions (monitoring vs advanced therapy) are often uncertain.
Limitations: Observational design limits causal inference and radiological clot localization assessment was not centrally adjudicated for reproducibility.

European journal of internal medicine

European practices for screening and follow-up in mixed connective tissue disease: a survey of clinicians from the European Federation of Internal Medicine and ERN ReCONNET

Chevalier K, Truchetet ME, Comarmond C et al. · 2026 Sep 22
Study Type: Cross-sectional survey
Key Question: How do European clinicians currently screen and monitor MCTD patients for ILD and pulmonary hypertension, given the absence of disease-specific guidelines?
Key Findings:
  • Baseline screening was widely performed: PFTs (91.1%), TTE (85.9%), HRCT (74.1%), NFC (71.9%) among 135 clinicians across 21 countries.
  • Follow-up practice was highly inconsistent: 61.5% used clinical judgement alone (rather than protocolised intervals) to guide repeat HRCT in ILD-negative patients, and 35.6% did not repeat NFC after a baseline scleroderma-like pattern.
  • PH referral pathways were variable, with 34.8% requiring DETECT algorithm validation before right heart catheterisation, despite DETECT lacking validation in MCTD.
Clinical Relevance: Acute medicine clinicians managing MCTD patients presenting with respiratory or cardiac symptoms should be aware that monitoring practices are unstandardised across Europe, increasing the risk of delayed detection of ILD/PH—leading causes of mortality—until MCTD-specific guidelines are developed.
Limitations: Survey-based design relying on self-reported practice introduces response and selection bias, and does not capture actual patient outcomes.

European journal of internal medicine

Circulating angiogenic and inflammatory biomarkers profiling in patients with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia

Villanueva B, Figueras A, Torres-Iglesias R et al. · 2026 Sep 23
Study Type: Exploratory prospective observational cohort study (matched case-control design)
Key Question: Can circulating angiogenic/inflammatory biomarkers help monitor bevacizumab response and quantify disease severity in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT)?
Key Findings:
  • VEGF-A rose markedly during bevacizumab therapy, making it unsuitable as a monitoring marker; PDGF and TSP1 rose significantly more in treatment responders vs non-responders.
  • At baseline, HHT patients (n=9) showed lower BMP9, sENG, sVEGFR1, sVEGFR2, VEGFc, TSP1, TSP2, and PDGF, but higher IL-6, compared to controls.
  • VEGFc, sVEGFR1, and TSP1 correlated positively with haemoglobin, suggesting utility as anaemia severity markers.
Clinical Relevance: These biomarkers may eventually help UK HHT centres objectively monitor bevacizumab response and disease burden, supporting individualised treatment decisions in this NHS-commissioned specialist service.
Limitations: Very small sample size (n=9 treated patients) limits statistical power and generalisability; findings require external validation.

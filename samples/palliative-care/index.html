Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Methylphenidate for cancer-related fatigue by assessment time: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Magro YZ, Fréz AR, Furlani VS et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (14 trials, n=1,155); time-specific and subgroup analyses were post hoc.
Key Question: Does methylphenidate improve cancer-related fatigue compared with placebo, and does any effect vary by assessment timing?
Key Findings:
  • Small improvement during treatment (SMD 0.26, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.47; I²=30%).
  • No significant benefit immediately post-treatment (SMD 0.27, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.53) or at follow-up (SMD 0.36, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.84); no significant quality of life improvement.
  • Exploratory subgroups showed significant effects with treatment ≥2 weeks, in advanced cancer/palliative care populations, and with racemic methylphenidate.
Clinical Relevance: Supports time-limited trials of methylphenidate (off-label for this indication in the UK) in advanced cancer, with realistic expectations of a modest effect that may not persist after stopping.
Limitations: Evidence certainty was low to very low, and the post hoc subgroup findings are hypothesis-generating only.

Supportive Care in Cancer

Prophylactic dexamethasone for prevention of opioid-induced nausea and vomiting in cancer patients: a randomised controlled trial (DEX-OINV Trial)

Sulfiker S, Mishra S, A AR et al. · 2026 Nov 07
Study Type: Open-label, single-centre RCT (n=150).
Key Question: Does prophylactic oral dexamethasone reduce nausea and vomiting when opioid-naïve cancer patients start oral morphine?
Key Findings:
  • Mean nausea NRS was lower with dexamethasone at all timepoints (Day 1: 1.36 vs 2.93; Day 5: 0.74 vs 1.53; p ≤ 0.01). Day 1 NNT was 5 to prevent NRS ≥4 nausea (aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.19–0.75).
  • Vomiting episodes were significantly reduced on Day 1.
  • No serious adverse events or discontinuations. The abstract gives no data on rescue antiemetic use.
Clinical Relevance: Offers a cheap, widely available option for early OINV prophylaxis, although current UK practice generally favours treating symptomatic OINV rather than routine prophylaxis, and these data are not yet practice-changing.
Limitations: Open-label, single-centre design with a subjective primary outcome risks performance and detection bias, and dexamethasone's own antiemetic and mood-altering effects and short follow-up limit generalisability.

Supportive Care in Cancer

Dynamic task-based rehabilitation and time to loss of toilet ambulation in a palliative care unit: a retrospective cohort study

Okamoto R, Okamoto Y, Jyoyama S et al. · 2026 Nov 07
Study Type: Single-centre retrospective cohort study (Japan)
Key Question: Is dynamic-task rehabilitation associated with a longer time to loss of toilet ambulation in patients with advanced cancer admitted to a palliative care unit?
Key Findings:
  • Among 205 patients who died as inpatients (≥14-day stay), median time to loss of toilet ambulation was 27 days with dynamic-task rehabilitation, 20 days with bedside-only rehabilitation, and 14 days with no rehabilitation.
  • Adjusted HR for ambulation loss versus no rehabilitation was 0.46 (95% CI 0.32–0.67) for dynamic-task rehabilitation; bedside-only showed no significant effect (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.56–1.23).
  • Competing-risk analysis treating death as a competing event gave consistent results (sHR 0.46, 95% CI 0.33–0.65).
Clinical Relevance: Supports considering active, functionally oriented rehabilitation rather than bedside-only input for maintaining toileting independence, a key dignity-related outcome, in UK inpatient hospice and palliative care settings.
Limitations: Retrospective design with likely confounding by indication and immortal time bias, as fitter, more motivated patients were probably selected for dynamic-task rehabilitation, so causality cannot be inferred.

Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Opioid Switching for Cancer Pain in Non-Hospitalized Patients: The challenges. A Narrative Review

Mercadante S, Bruera E · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Narrative review (non-systematic) of clinical trials and predominantly retrospective observational studies.
Key Question: How safe and effective is opioid switching for cancer pain in community settings (outpatient and home care), and when is inpatient admission needed?
Key Findings:
  • Community opioid switching success rates were 65%–82.5%, mainly for uncontrolled pain (52%–88% of switches). Rotation to transdermal fentanyl, levorphanol, buprenorphine and tiered oral methadone was feasible.
  • Published equianalgesic ratios varied widely. Post-switch doses were generally not lower than pre-switch doses, though this may partly reflect disease progression.
  • Acute pain crises, severe neurotoxicity and high-dose methadone rotations warrant inpatient palliative monitoring. The SPOT digital tool reported 96.3% equianalgesic accuracy, but telemedicine-guided protocols lack prospective validation.
Clinical Relevance: Supports community-led switching for uncomplicated cases by experienced teams, which is relevant to UK community palliative services where most cancer deaths occur outside hospital.
Limitations: Narrative methodology with no stated search strategy or quality appraisal, and heavy reliance on retrospective data, limits reliability and generalisability.

Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Perceptions of living with drug-resistant epilepsy and the potential role of neuropalliative care

Hixon AM, Drees C, Lopez-Esquibel N et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Qualitative study (semi-structured interviews with thematic analysis; n=20) with supplementary medical record data extraction.
Key Question: How do adults with drug-resistant epilepsy perceive their quality of life, and would they welcome palliative or neuropalliative care involvement?
Key Findings:
  • Participants reported substantial effects on self-identity, family and social life; main concerns were loss of independence and uncertainty. Close relationships and faith were the main sources of strength.
  • 90% wanted more information from clinicians (diagnosis, medical/surgical options, psychosocial issues); 80% had no advance directive.
  • Only 20% could define palliative care beforehand; after explanation, about half considered it useful, particularly during seizure deterioration, pre-surgery, or major life decisions.
Clinical Relevance: Supports scoping neuropalliative input for epilepsy in UK services, particularly for communication, symptom burden, and advance care planning, although this is a non-cancer population with limited existing palliative pathways.
Limitations: Small single-population sample, likely setting- and selection-bound, with no assessment of whether palliative care involvement improves outcomes. The authors acknowledge that further research is needed.

Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Palliative Tracheostomy Decision-Making: A Targeted Scoping Review

Gurcan O, Gabela A, Jalfon R et al. · 2026 Nov 02
Study Type: Targeted scoping review (single database, PubMed/MEDLINE; 4 sources included)
Key Question: How are decisions to place a tracheostomy with palliative (non-life-prolonging) intent made, and what factors, stakeholders and ethical issues shape them?
Key Findings:
  • Of 183 records screened, only four met criteria: two adult case reports, one paediatric case series and one infant ethics analysis. None formally defined palliative tracheostomy.
  • Goals were patient- or parent-defined (communication, time with family, discharge home/hospice). In three sources, achieving the goal depended on community or hospice care the hospital did not provide; only one weighed this before offering the procedure. Caregiver burden featured in one source.
  • No source followed patients after placement to revisit the tracheostomy or ventilation.
Clinical Relevance: In UK practice, where community or hospice capacity for tracheostomy care varies, these findings support confirming discharge support is achievable, discussing withdrawal with comfort care as an alternative, and documenting the stated purpose with agreed triggers for review.
Limitations: Evidence base is minimal (four anecdotal sources, one database), so the recommendations are hypothesis-generating rather than evidence-based.

Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Cancer versus Non-Cancer Patients Admitted to Hospice: a comparison study

Mercadante S, Travia M, Scimone P et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Prospective observational single-centre cohort study (175 consecutive hospice admissions over one year).
Key Question: How do non-cancer and cancer patients differ in characteristics, symptom burden, nutritional support, length of stay and outcome within one residential hospice?
Key Findings:
  • Non-cancer patients (n=24, 13.7%; 21/24 neurological) were older (median 78.5 vs 72 years, q=0.012), with lower KPS (20 vs 30, q=0.006) and higher MDAS scores (13 vs 4, uncorrected P=0.021).
  • Artificial nutrition was more common in non-cancer patients (37.5% vs 14.9%) but did not survive multiple-comparison correction (q=0.060).
  • Length of stay (median 11 vs 8 days) and in-hospice mortality (70.8% vs 80.0%) did not differ significantly. No ESAS item differed between groups after correction.
Clinical Relevance: Non-cancer patients, particularly those with neurological disease, may reach hospice later and with greater functional and cognitive impairment, which is relevant to referral timing and equity of access in UK hospice and community palliative services.
Limitations: About half of oncological and three quarters of non-oncological patients lacked ESAS data (one valid non-cancer patient by T7), and the non-cancer group was small, so symptom burden comparisons are unreliable.

Journal of Pain and Symptom Management

Palliative Care Understanding Among U.S. Adults: Findings from HINTS 7

Cafferty LA, Lupu DE, Bingenheimer JB et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Cross-sectional survey analysis (nationally representative US data, HINTS 7 Cycle 1, 2024)
Key Question: Are caregiving experience and personal history of serious illness associated with public understanding of palliative care?
Key Findings:
  • Caregivers had nearly twice the odds of adequate palliative care understanding compared with non-caregivers (exact OR and CI not given in the abstract).
  • Self-reported serious health condition, including cancer, heart disease and lung disease, was not associated with understanding or awareness; no significant interaction with caregiving.
  • Adjusted model included 6,128 complete cases from 7,278 respondents.
Clinical Relevance: Patients with serious illness appear no better informed about palliative care than the general public, suggesting missed opportunities for early, explicit introduction of palliative care, which is relevant to NHS referral and advance care planning pathways despite the US setting.
Limitations: Cross-sectional, self-reported design precludes causal inference, and US findings may not transfer to the UK healthcare context.

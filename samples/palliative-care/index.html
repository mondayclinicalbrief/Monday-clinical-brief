BMJ supportive & palliative care

How can speech and language therapists help decision-making on clinically assisted hydration for adults at the end of life?

Mizzi L · 2026 Jul 20
Study Type: Commentary/narrative overview
Key Question: What role can speech and language therapists (SLTs) play in supporting decision-making around clinically assisted hydration (CAH) for dying adults?
Key Findings:
  • No clinical consensus exists on the risks/benefits of CAH in the last week of life, supporting a person-centred, individualised approach.
  • SLTs can differentiate symptoms related to dysphagia versus dying, optimise comfortable oral intake, and support oral hygiene.
  • SLTs facilitate informed decision-making by enhancing communication between patients, families, and the multidisciplinary team.
Clinical Relevance: UK palliative care MDTs should consider formally integrating SLT expertise into CAH decision-making to strengthen person-centred, ethically sound end-of-life care in line with NHS best-practice guidance.
Limitations: As a commentary rather than primary research, it offers expert opinion without new empirical data.

BMJ supportive & palliative care

Disadvantaged ethnic minority communities and legalising assisted dying: coercion, trust and equity in end-of-life care

Hussain JA, Jacob I, Khalid R et al. · 2026 Jul 21
Study Type: Qualitative study (community consultations)
Key Question: How do disadvantaged ethnic minority communities in a UK city view proposed assisted dying legislation, and what are the implications for equity in end-of-life care?
Key Findings:
  • Consultations with 137 participants across three groups (ethnic minority adults in deprived areas, women with domestic abuse experience, parents of autistic/learning-disabled young adults) revealed simultaneous compassion for assisted dying requests and significant concern about risks to marginalised groups.
  • Four key themes: mistrust of healthcare systems; direct/indirect and structural coercion risks; concerns over capacity, decision-making and family involvement; potential erosion of trust in palliative care services.
Clinical Relevance: As the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill progresses through England and Wales, palliative care clinicians must anticipate how assisted dying legislation could disproportionately affect trust and engagement among ethnically diverse and deprived populations already experiencing end-of-life inequalities.
Limitations: Findings are based on a single UK city with specific demographic characteristics, limiting generalisability to other regions or communities.

Journal of pain and symptom management

The Challenges of Compassion Bias in Clinical Decision-Making: A Case-Based Ethical Analysis

Vellozzi-Averhoff C, Oltman C, Sundaramoorthy A et al. · 2026 Jul 20
Study Type: Case-based ethical analysis/commentary
Key Question: How does well-intentioned clinician empathy ("compassion bias") distort decision-making and undermine patient autonomy in serious illness care?
Key Findings:
  • Introduces "compassion bias": clinicians' assumptions about quality of life and suffering led to deferred enteral nutrition in a young woman with PML-related neurologic decline, without adequate surrogate engagement.
  • This paternalistic approach likely shortened the patient's life and delayed exploration of family values regarding life-prolonging treatment.
  • Compassion bias is shown to interact with cognitive heuristics, implicit bias, and moral distress, contributing to unrecognised ethical drift in care.
Clinical Relevance: UK palliative care and ethics teams should be alert to how empathy-driven assumptions—not just negative stereotyping—can compromise shared decision-making, particularly in capacity-limited or surrogate-decision contexts common in NHS complex neurological care.
Limitations: Single-case analysis limits generalisability; conclusions are conceptual/ethical rather than empirically tested.

Journal of pain and symptom management

Small-Count Suppression Substantially Distorts Estimates of Pediatric Opioid Utilization in Japan

Abe K, Sugiyama M, Miyazaki K et al. · 2026 Jul 21
Study Type: Retrospective analysis of national administrative database (Japan NDB Open Data), FY2018–2023
Key Question: Does small-count data suppression distort estimates of pediatric opioid prescribing trends derived from Japan's national claims database?
Key Findings:
  • Suppression of strong opioid formulation data (counts <1,000 coded as non-disclosed) rose progressively, reaching 100% by FY2023.
  • Sensitivity analysis imputing suppressed values showed a 4.9-fold discrepancy versus observed data in FY2022.
  • Primary (unadjusted) analysis suggested an apparent decline in pediatric opioid utilization from 1,826 to 346 mg MME/100,000 population (FY2018–2023), likely a data artefact rather than true trend.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights a methodological pitfall relevant to UK clinicians and researchers using small-population or suppressed administrative datasets (e.g., NHS Digital/OpenPrescribing) to monitor rare paediatric palliative prescribing trends, cautioning against misinterpreting apparent declines as reduced clinical need.
Limitations: Findings are specific to Japan's NDB suppression methodology and pediatric opioid formulations, limiting direct generalisability to other national datasets.

Journal of pain and symptom management

Child, Caregiver, and Nurse Symptom Reports in Oncology: Evidence for Prioritizing the Child's Voice

Sadler K, Alharbi MS, Althaqafi W et al. · 2026 Jul 21
Study Type: Multiphase mixed-methods study (quantitative triad comparison with qualitative interviews)
Key Question: How well do family caregiver and nurse symptom reports align with children's own symptom self-reports in paediatric oncology/HSCT populations?
Key Findings:
  • 90 child–caregiver–nurse triads assessed using SSPedi; most frequently reported child symptoms were reduced appetite (11.1%) and nausea/vomiting (10%).
  • Statistically significant agreement was found for 12/15 symptoms (p<.05), but correlations were generally weak.
  • Family caregivers tended to over-report symptoms; children often under-disclosed discomfort; proxies relied more on observation than direct child input.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights a systematic mismatch between proxy and child-reported symptom burden, reinforcing the need for validated, child-completed tools (e.g., SSPedi) rather than sole reliance on caregiver/nurse observation in UK paediatric palliative oncology practice.
Limitations: Single-country (Saudi Arabia), two-centre sample limits generalisability to UK/NHS paediatric populations and healthcare contexts.

Journal of pain and symptom management

Internet images of palliative care: a visual content analysis

Buenrostro AR, Khorramak K, Dent E et al. · 2026 Jul 21
Study Type: Visual content analysis (cross-sectional, descriptive)
Key Question: How is palliative care visually depicted in internet images, and does this reinforce misconceptions about the specialty?
Key Findings:
  • Analysis of 335 unique images (from 369 websites, mainly US 46.9% and Canada 24.9%) identified four dominant themes: hand-holding (60.6%), enclosed/indoor settings (94.7%), cheerful comfort care, and passive dependency.
  • Patients were overwhelmingly older adults shown lying/sitting (only 1.3% standing), versus healthcare providers who were standing in 43.3% of images.
  • Most hospital-based images depicted bedside inpatient scenes (70.1%); only 6.0% showed outpatient settings.
Clinical Relevance: UK clinicians seeking to promote earlier referral and integration of palliative care alongside disease-modifying treatment should be aware that prevailing internet imagery visually reinforces the "end-of-life only" stereotype, potentially deterring timely patient and public engagement, relevant to NHS efforts to broaden access to specialist palliative care earlier in illness trajectories.
Limitations: Findings are based on a single search date and location (Toronto, 2022) using specific search terms, limiting generalisability to current or region-specific (e.g., UK) internet imagery.

Journal of pain and symptom management

Insurance Type and Quality of End-of-Life Care in Adults < 65 Years with Hematologic Malignancies

Raman HS, Packnett E, Banach E et al. · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Retrospective population-based cohort study (US claims data)
Key Question: Does insurance type (Medicaid vs commercial) influence quality of end-of-life care in younger adults (<65) with haematologic malignancies?
Key Findings:
  • Overall EOL care quality was poor: 58.2% had high-intensity healthcare utilisation, 48.7% died in hospital, only 26.0% used hospice and 27.3% received palliative care.
  • Commercial insurance was associated with higher odds of high-intensity utilisation (OR 1.69, 95%CI 1.44–1.99) and in-hospital death (OR 2.71, 95%CI 2.29–3.21) versus Medicaid.
  • Commercially insured patients had lower odds of hospice enrolment (OR 0.70) and palliative care use (OR 0.76) compared with Medicaid beneficiaries.
Clinical Relevance: Although US-based, these findings challenge assumptions that better insurance/resource access improves EOL outcomes, prompting UK clinicians to consider how funding/referral pathways (e.g., variable specialist palliative care access across ICBs) may similarly drive inequitable high-intensity care at end of life in haematology patients.
Limitations: Retrospective claims-based design lacks clinical granularity (e.g., symptom burden, patient preferences) and findings may not generalise beyond the US insurance system.

Loneliness prevalence and risk factors in the last year of life: analysis of UK household panel data

Maun E, Gardiner C, McConnell T et al. · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Retrospective cohort analysis (secondary analysis of UK Household Longitudinal Study panel data)
Key Question: How common is loneliness in the last year of life among UK adults, and what factors predict it?
Key Findings:
  • 32.2% (95% CI 27.2–37.6) of adults reported loneliness within their last 12 months of life, based on UCLA-Loneliness scale scores ≥6.
  • No significant change in prevalence between 2017 and 2022, though methodological survey changes limit confidence in this comparison.
  • Independent risk factors (fully adjusted): probable mental health condition, unmarried status, and having ≥3 functional impairments.
Clinical Relevance: These findings support routine screening for loneliness in end-of-life care pathways across NHS and community palliative services, particularly targeting unmarried patients, those with mental health comorbidities, and those with significant functional decline.
Limitations: Loneliness was measured only once within the last year of life, with no data on timing relative to death, limiting insight into how loneliness evolves as death approaches.

Active surveillance for intermediate-risk prostate cancer: a systematic review and pooled analysis

Uleri A, Cella L, Long-Depaquit T et al. · 2026 Jul 26
Study Type: Systematic review and pooled (meta-)analysis
Key Question: How safe and durable is active surveillance (AS) as management for selected intermediate-risk (Gleason Grade Group 1–2) prostate cancer?
Key Findings:
  • Pooled AS discontinuation-free survival: 70% (95% CI 62–78) at 3 years, 63% (95% CI 58–68) at 5 years, 53% (95% CI 38–67) at 10 years; GG2-only cohorts fared worse (40% at 10 years).
  • GG≥3 upgrade-free survival was 84% at 3 years and 78% at 5 years overall, improving to 92% and 87% respectively when mpMRI was used for selection/follow-up.
  • No significant difference in discontinuation between GG1 and GG2 (HR 1.08, 95% CI 0.86–1.35).
Clinical Relevance: Supports cautious extension of AS to selected favourable intermediate-risk patients within UK pathways, provided mpMRI-based selection and surveillance protocols (as per NICE/BAUS guidance) are used to mitigate upgrading risk.
Limitations: Moderate risk of bias and substantial heterogeneity across AS protocols, with limited long-term oncological outcome data.

AI-based anatomical recognition for bladder neck dissection in robot-assisted radical prostatectomy

Fujiwara S, Fukumoto K, Takeuchi M et al. · 2026 Jul 26
Study Type: Model development and validation study (retrospective, technical/AI performance evaluation)
Key Question: Can an AI-based segmentation model accurately identify key anatomical structures (prostate, bladder, retrotrigonal layer) during bladder neck dissection in RARP?
Key Findings:
  • Initial DeepLabV3+ model IoU: 0.41 (prostate), 0.44 (bladder); after active-learning retraining, IoU improved to 0.75 and 0.68 respectively.
  • Performance was maintained on an independent test set of 202 frames from seven different surgeons, suggesting generalisability across operators.
  • Model was trained on 1353 annotated frames from 25 of 210 total RARP videos across two institutions.
Clinical Relevance: Intraoperative AI-guided anatomical recognition could support safer bladder neck dissection and structured trainee education in robotic urology programmes, relevant to NHS robotic surgery training pathways and standardisation efforts.
Limitations: Small annotated training/test dataset with no reported clinical outcome correlation (e.g., margin status, continence), limiting evidence of real-world surgical benefit.

Key Question: Is intravesical atezolizumab (via passive instillation or direct sub-urothelial injection) safe and feasible in bladder cancer patients undergoing radical cystectomy, including those unsuitable for cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy?
Key Findings:
  • No outcome data yet reported; this is a protocol describing planned dose-escalation cohorts (600 mg or 1200 mg, single or weekly x3–6 doses) across two delivery routes.
  • Primary endpoints: dose-limiting toxicities and adverse events; secondary endpoints include progression-free survival, pathological complete response, and compliance.
  • Exploratory work includes pharmacokinetic profiling and imaging mass spectrometry to map drug distribution within cystectomy specimens.

A stent-plus-irrigation protocol after adolescent hypospadias repair: a multicentre randomised controlled trial

Ye Z, Chen H, Zhang Q et al. · 2026 Jul 27
Study Type: Multicentre randomised controlled trial
Key Question: Does adding a small-calibre urethral stent plus twice-daily saline irrigation, versus standard catheter drainage alone, reduce complications after adolescent hypospadias repair?
Key Findings:
  • Overall postoperative complication rate significantly lower with stent-plus-irrigation (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.20–0.56; P<0.001).
  • Urethral fistula rate reduced (RR 0.27, 95% CI 0.14–0.51; P<0.001).
  • Surgical site infection rate reduced (RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.21–0.87; P=0.020).
Clinical Relevance: Adolescent hypospadias repair carries significant fistula/SSI morbidity; this low-cost, technically simple adjunct could be readily adopted in NHS paediatric/adolescent urology practice to improve outcomes and reduce re-operation burden.
Limitations: The two-arm design combines stent and irrigation, so the independent contribution of each component cannot be determined.

Small- and large-calibre ureteroscopes: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis

Di Bello F, Verri P, Bravo-Balado A et al. · 2026 Jul 28
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis (8 studies: 4 RCTs, 4 retrospective; includes sensitivity analyses)
Key Question: Do small-calibre ureteroscopes (≤6.3/7.5 F) achieve comparable perioperative outcomes to large-calibre ureteroscopes (≥7.5/9.8 F) in stone treatment?
Key Findings:
  • Comparing 6.3 vs 7.5 F scopes: no significant difference in stone-free rate (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92–1.06) or overall complications (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.77–1.51).
  • Operative time trended shorter with small-calibre scopes but was not statistically significant (SMD −14.5 min, 95% CI −33.4 to 3.3).
  • No differences found between 7.5 F and >7.5 F comparisons; sensitivity analyses (flexible-only, RCTs-only) confirmed main results.
Clinical Relevance: Supports growing UK adoption of smaller-calibre ureteroscopes, suggesting non-inferiority in efficacy/safety while potentially reducing ureteric trauma and enabling wider access without compromising stone clearance—relevant to NHS day-case flexible ureteroscopy pathways.
Limitations: Substantial heterogeneity in scope calibre definitions and study design (mix of RCTs and retrospective cohorts) limits strength of conclusions; adequately powered RCTs are needed.

Retrograde intrarenal surgery with flexible and navigable suction access sheaths vs mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy for large upper urinary tract stones: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Lee HJ, Yaow C, Chong RIH et al. · 2026 Jul 29
Study Type: Systematic review and meta-analysis (10 studies: 3 RCTs, 7 retrospective cohorts; n=2347)
Key Question: For large renal stones (predominantly 2–3 cm), how does FANS-RIRS compare with mini-PCNL in terms of stone clearance and perioperative outcomes?
Key Findings:
  • Stone-free rates were comparable between FANS-RIRS and mini-PCNL overall (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.70–1.17) and for stones ≥2 cm (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.60–1.08), with low heterogeneity.
  • Ancillary procedure rates did not differ significantly (OR 1.22, 95% CI 0.61–2.44); urosepsis rates were low in both groups.
  • FANS-RIRS showed fewer overall complications, smaller haemoglobin drop, less transfusion need, and shorter hospital stay, though mini-PCNL had shorter operative times for stones ≥2 cm.
Clinical Relevance: These findings challenge current guideline preference for PCNL in stones >2 cm and support FANS-RIRS as a safe, effective alternative that could reduce inpatient stay and transfusion burden—relevant to NHS efforts to shift stone surgery towards day-case/ambulatory pathways.
Limitations: Predominantly Asian, non-randomised studies with variable practice patterns limit generalisability and introduce potential bias.

Oncologic outcomes of radical radiotherapy in frail patients with upper tract urothelial carcinoma

Chen WP, Yap WK, Luo HL et al. · 2026 Jul 31
Study Type: Retrospective multicentre cohort study (IPTW-adjusted)
Key Question: Does higher-dose definitive radiotherapy improve oncologic outcomes compared with lower-dose radiotherapy in medically frail UTUC patients unsuitable for surgery?
Key Findings:
  • High-dose RT (BED10 ≥74.3 Gy) achieved markedly better disease control than low-dose RT (98% vs 62%, P<0.001) and improved 3-year overall survival (46% vs 11%, P<0.001).
  • High-dose RT also improved 3-year locoregional control (51% vs 29%), freedom from distant metastasis (75% vs 39%), and freedom from progression (43% vs 25%) (all P<0.05).
  • Higher RT dose independently predicted improved FFP (aHR 0.96) and OS (aHR 0.93); pre-RT gross haematuria and CCI >5 independently predicted worse OS.
Clinical Relevance: For UK MDTs managing frail UTUC patients unfit for nephroureterectomy, this supports considering dose-escalated radical radiotherapy as a viable, potentially curative alternative rather than palliative-intent treatment alone.
Limitations: Retrospective design with residual confounding despite IPTW adjustment, and small sample size (n=63) across heterogeneous institutions limits generalisability.

What Comes After Matters: The Importance of Postprogression Care in Patients with Metastatic Hormone-sensitive Prostate Cancer

Mortezavi A, van der Hulle T, van den Bergh R · 2026 Jul 23
Study Type: Editorial/commentary (no primary data presented).
Key Question: Does the survival benefit seen with upfront combination therapy in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) reflect true treatment superiority, or partly undertreatment of control arms after progression?
Key Findings:
  • Raises concern that many mHSPC trials do not mandate or standardise postprogression therapy in control arms, potentially inflating apparent benefits of upfront combination regimens.
  • Argues that without controlled treatment sequencing, it is difficult to distinguish true biological superiority from suboptimal comparator management.
  • Calls for trial designs with prespecified postprogression treatment pathways to enable evaluation of de-escalation strategies.
Clinical Relevance: UK clinicians interpreting mHSPC trial data (informing NICE/NHS treatment pathways) should consider whether control-arm postprogression care was adequate before assuming combination therapy's survival advantage is unequivocal.
Limitations: As an opinion piece, it offers no new empirical evidence, relying on interpretive critique of existing trial designs.

Postoperative pain outcomes following minor oral surgery in opioid tolerant patients: a retrospective cohort study

Gopinath A, Ayeni FE, Cox S · 2026 Oct 21
Study Type: Single-centre retrospective cohort study (1,263 patient records, 2020-2024).
Key Question: Does standard postoperative analgesia (paracetamol plus NSAIDs) provide adequate pain control after minor oral surgery in opioid tolerant patients compared with opioid naïve patients?
Key Findings:
  • Opioid tolerant patients (n=184) returned needing additional opioid analgesia far more often than opioid naïve patients (n=1,079): 28.8% vs 2.2%.
  • Adjusted OR 24.59 (95% CI 13.26-45.62; p<0.001), consistent across demographic subgroups and extraction types.
Clinical Relevance: Standard non-opioid regimens often fail in opioid tolerant patients, so UK practitioners and oral surgery services should identify these patients pre-operatively and plan tailored analgesia, ideally with the patient's prescriber or addiction service.
Limitations: Retrospective, single-centre design, with return for opioid analgesia as a proxy outcome (not measured pain scores), so under-reporting and unmeasured confounding are likely; the study also does not test any alternative analgesic strategy.

British Dental Journal

A poorly differentiated salivary carcinoma NOS of the sublingual gland initially presenting as sialolithiasis: implications for early re-assessment in clinical practice

Ramnarine R, McDonnell F, Korsen J · 2026 Oct
Study Type: Case report (single patient).
Key Question: Can a floor-of-mouth swelling presenting as a salivary calculus conceal a rare malignancy, and when should the working diagnosis be re-assessed?
Key Findings:
  • A 31-year-old woman with a hard floor-of-mouth swelling was initially managed as sialolithiasis, though plain radiography showed no radiopacity.
  • Three months later she developed lower anterior tooth mobility and progressive swelling. CT showed a 27 mm anterior mandibular defect.
  • Diagnosis was T4a N1 M1 poorly differentiated sublingual gland carcinoma (NOS) with lung, brain and spine metastases. She died ten months after diagnosis.
Clinical Relevance: A hard floor-of-mouth swelling without radiographic evidence of a stone, or with progressive symptoms such as tooth mobility, warrants prompt re-evaluation and urgent referral under UK suspected cancer pathways.
Limitations: A single case cannot establish how often such presentations occur, and the abstract does not clarify whether the ultrasound finding truly supported a calculus.

Evidence-Based Dentistry

Do hydrophilic implant surfaces provide a clinically meaningful advantage?

Afrashtehfar KI, Jurado CA · 2026 Oct 23
Study Type: Commentary on a multicentre, split-mouth randomised controlled trial (68 patients, 136 implants, 1-year follow-up).
Key Question: Does a hydrophilic SLActive implant surface give better peri-implant outcomes than a conventional SLA surface in healed posterior sites after one year of loading?
Key Findings:
  • Primary outcome (marginal bone level change, loading to 12 months): -0.04 mm (SLActive) vs -0.07 mm (SLA); difference 0.03 mm (95% CI -0.09 to 0.14; p = 0.658).
  • Bone remodelling from placement to 12 months was slightly greater with SLActive (0.20 vs 0.06 mm; difference 0.15 mm; 95% CI 0.02-0.27), favouring SLA.
  • Post-loading survival was 100% for both. Early failure was 95.5% vs 97.0% survival. ISQ, probing depth, bleeding on probing, mucositis, and OHIP-14 were comparable, with no peri-implantitis.
Clinical Relevance: For UK practitioners weighing the cost of premium hydrophilic implants, this suggests no demonstrable one-year advantage in routine, healed-site cases.
Limitations: Follow-up was only one year in a low-risk, selected population, so conclusions cannot extend to compromised sites, early or immediate loading, or long-term outcomes.

Clinical Oral Investigations

Impact of different post-polymerization protocols on fracture load and two-body wear of 3D-printed fixed dental prostheses

Gekle S, Stawarczyk B, Mayinger F et al. · 2026 Oct 22
Study Type: In vitro laboratory study (360 DLP-printed mandibular molar crowns; three-way ANOVA)
Key Question: How do different light post-polymerisation protocols and ageing affect the fracture load and wear of 3D-printed permanent and temporary crown resins?
Key Findings:
  • Post-curing protocol and material significantly affected fracture load (both p < 0.001), but the best protocol varied by resin: 460 nm LED gave the highest initial values for two resins (e.g. 1505 ± 251 N and 1910 ± 237 N), while a two-capacitor flashlight was best for the third (1783 ± 333 N).
  • Ageing had no overall effect on fracture load (p = 0.100), though individual material-protocol combinations rose (2 of 15) or fell (3 of 15).
  • Two-body wear (−0.16 to −0.35 mm) did not differ between protocols or materials.
Clinical Relevance: Practices or labs printing crowns in-house should follow resin-specific post-curing instructions, as one protocol does not suit all materials.
Limitations: Being in vitro, results may not translate to clinical performance, and some individual specimens failed below reported maximum molar bite forces.

Clinical Oral Investigations

Influence of post-curing duration on degree of conversion, microhardness, and surface roughness of three commercially available 3D-printed permanent crown resins

Abdulai D, Sasany R, Özcan M · 2026 Oct 28
Study Type: In vitro laboratory study (180 disc specimens, three resins, three post-curing durations).
Key Question: Does post-curing duration (5, 30 or 60 minutes) affect the degree of conversion, hardness and surface roughness of 3D-printed permanent crown resins?
Key Findings:
  • Longer post-curing significantly increased degree of conversion and Vickers hardness in all three resins (p < 0.001), but the size of the effect depended on the material. Saremco Print CROWNTEC improved most; VarseoSmile TriniQ improved least.
  • Surface roughness was unaffected by material or post-curing time (p > 0.05) after standardised polishing.
  • Specific values, effect sizes and confidence intervals are not reported in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: Printed crowns in UK practice and labs may be under-cured if manufacturer or default post-curing times are used, so protocols should follow each resin's validated parameters.
Limitations: Flat discs tested in vitro, with no data on long-term clinical performance such as wear, fracture or biocompatibility.

J. E. H. Duckworth MC LDS RCS Eng (1897-1957) and the London Group Circular: politics, polemics and prophecies

Hellyer P · 2026 Oct
Study Type: Historical biographical analysis (narrative, based on archival writings). Not original clinical research.
Key Question: What role did J. E. H. Duckworth and his London Group Circular play in dental professional politics, and what arguments were made against a national dental service in the 1940s?
Key Findings:
  • Duckworth, a decorated WWI veteran and North London practitioner, became a leading figure in the wartime Group Movement, which grew from local committees supporting dentists called up for military service.
  • The Movement helped unify the three representative dental bodies of the time, yet Duckworth's monthly circular became a prominent voice against introducing a national dental service.
  • The paper draws on his writings to set out the case made against a dental NHS. The abstract does not detail the specific arguments.
Clinical Relevance: It offers historical context for the profession's divided response to the founding of NHS dentistry, which still shapes debates on NHS dental contracts and access.
Limitations: Not applicable to this study type; the abstract gives no detail on sources or method, and findings rest largely on one individual's publications, which may not represent wider professional opinion.

British Dental Journal

Communication phrases to support trauma-informed care in oral health: a concise review

Choi KS, Sohn W, Leadbeatter D · 2026 Oct
Study Type: Concise narrative literature review (six databases plus grey literature; no formal quality appraisal or meta-analysis reported).
Key Question: What communication phrases can oral health practitioners use to deliver trauma-informed care (TIC)?
Key Findings:
  • Of 167 papers and nine grey literature articles identified, 27 addressed TIC in oral health; only eight contained usable communication phrases.
  • Phrases were grouped by clinical situation: before treatment, screening for trauma, during treatment, recognising signs and symptoms of trauma, responding to disclosure, active listening, and motivational interviewing.
  • The authors conclude the phrases could serve as a practical tool but have not been tested for effectiveness.
Clinical Relevance: Provides ready-to-use scripts for sensitive consultations, useful for UK teams who see patients with trauma histories but have little formal TIC training.
Limitations: The evidence base is very small (eight papers) and the phrases are unvalidated, so there is no evidence yet that they improve patient outcomes or experience.

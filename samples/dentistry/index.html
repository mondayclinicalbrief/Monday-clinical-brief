John Walford McLean - the dental innovator who led the British development of resin composites

Fleming GJP · 2026 Aug
Study Type: Historical commentary/biographical article.
Key Question: Not applicable — this is a biographical account of John Walford McLean's contributions to British dental materials research rather than an original research question.
Key Findings:
  • McLean contributed to major UK dental materials innovations between 1950–1983, including aluminous core porcelain (1965), reinforced porcelains, polyacrylic acid-based cements, glass-ionomer cements and epoxy resin systems.
  • In 1984, despite decades of innovation, McLean regarded only gold and porcelain as truly permanent restorative materials.
  • In 1980 he predicted that future progress in aesthetic dentistry would depend on new technologies rather than material substitution — a view the author endorses as prescient.
Clinical Relevance: Understanding this history contextualises the evolution of restorative materials still used in UK general dental practice today, including glass-ionomer cements and resin composites.
Limitations: As a historical/biographical commentary, it presents no primary clinical data or systematic evidence.

Study Type: Case series
Key Question: What patterns and management challenges arise from traumatic dental injuries associated with e-bike or e-scooter use?
Key Findings:
  • Three adult cases presented with combination TDIs including concussion, enamel-dentine fractures, luxations, alveolar bone fractures, and avulsions.
  • Contributing factors identified include mechanism of trauma, alcohol intake, and inconsistent helmet use.
  • Cases highlighted challenges such as delayed presentation and need for multi-disciplinary dental management.
Clinical Relevance: With e-bike and e-scooter use rising in the UK, dental practitioners in primary care are increasingly likely to be the first point of contact for these often complex, multi-injury presentations, requiring awareness of appropriate assessment and referral pathways.
Limitations: As a small case series of only three patients, findings are descriptive and cannot establish injury prevalence or causal risk factors.

Key Findings:
  • Terminology used across microbiome studies is inconsistent and often ambiguous, complicating comparison and clinical translation of findings.
  • Current sequencing-based microbiome analyses cannot yet capture host immune response, functional gene expression, or strain-level differences that determine whether a microorganism is pathogenic.

Clinical Relevance: UK dental professionals should treat commercial or research claims about "microbiome testing" for diagnosis or risk prediction with caution, as the science is not yet mature enough to support routine clinical decision-making.
Limitations: As a narrative review without systematic methodology or primary data, conclusions reflect expert opinion rather than quantitative evidence synthesis.

Developmental enamel defects and hypomineralized second primary molars among preschool children in Kuwait: associations with caries severity and oral health-related quality of life

Alanzi A, Behzadi S, Baskaradoss J et al. · 2026 Aug 24
Study Type: Cross-sectional population-based study
Key Question: Are developmental enamel defects (DDEs) and hypomineralized second primary molars (HSPMs) in preschool children linked to caries severity and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL)?
Key Findings:
  • DDEs were found in 66.2% of children (combination defects 29.9%, demarcated opacities 25.5%); HSPMs affected 20.0%.
  • DDEs were associated with double the odds of caries (aOR 2.00, 95% CI 1.48–2.70); enamel hypoplasia showed the strongest link to caries (aOR 3.50) and untreated caries consequences (pufa>0, aOR 2.34); HSPMs also raised caries odds (aOR 2.42).
  • Enamel hypoplasia and severe HSPMs were significantly associated with poorer child and family OHRQoL scores (p<0.001–0.01).
Clinical Relevance: UK GDPs and paediatric dental teams should recognise DDEs and HSPMs as red flags for higher caries risk and reduced quality of life, supporting early preventive intervention in line with NHS paediatric oral health strategies.
Limitations: Cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between enamel defects and caries/OHRQoL outcomes.

Outcome and erosion of root-end filling materials following endodontic apical surgery: a minimum follow-up of 4 years

Hussain O, Haug SR · 2026 Aug 24
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study
Key Question: Does the choice of root-end filling material (IRM vs hydraulic calcium silicate cements) affect long-term healing and survival after apical surgery, and does material erosion influence outcomes?
Key Findings:
  • At mean 6.6-year follow-up, complete healing occurred in 61.3% of teeth; tooth survival was 89.1%, with no significant difference between IRM and HCSC.
  • Late failures occurred in 7.3% of initially successful cases, equally distributed between materials.
  • Radiographic erosion was seen in 14.6% of teeth (11.1% IRM vs 18.5% HCSC, not significant) and was associated with reduced likelihood of complete healing (OR 2.7, 95% CI 0.8–8.1).
Clinical Relevance: Both materials perform comparably long-term, but clinicians should be aware that late failures can occur and that root-end filling erosion—an underexplored phenomenon—may compromise healing, supporting the need for extended post-surgical review beyond typical 1-year follow-up.
Limitations: Retrospective design with substantial loss to final inclusion (137/336 teeth), limiting generalisability and introducing potential selection bias.

Schaffrath K, Kniha K, Ooms M et al. · 2026 Aug 25
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study
Key Question: Does implant stability at 3 months differ between implants placed in native bone versus one-stage autologous internal or external sinus floor elevation?
Key Findings:
  • Osseointegration rates were significantly higher for implants in original bone (94.4%) compared with external elevation (76.2%) and internal elevation (80.8%).
  • RFA values (implant stability quotient) were highest in the original bone group (77.5) versus external (75) and internal elevation (75) groups (p=0.030).
  • Authors recommend extending the healing period by at least 4 additional weeks for one-stage sinus elevation procedures.
Clinical Relevance: For UK clinicians offering implant-based rehabilitation of the atrophic posterior maxilla, this supports one-stage sinus elevation as a viable, shorter alternative to two-stage approaches, but with a longer healing interval before loading to reduce failure risk.
Limitations: Retrospective design with relatively small subgroup sizes (21–72 implants) limits generalisability and control for confounding factors.

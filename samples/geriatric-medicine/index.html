Age and ageing

Incidence and characteristics of poisonings in older adults with and without dementia: a matched cohort study using Swedish registry data

Wojt IR, Hoang MT, Wastesson JW et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Matched cohort study (retrospective, registry-based)
Key Question: Does dementia diagnosis increase the incidence and change the nature of poisoning events in older adults compared with matched controls?
Key Findings:
  • Poisoning incidence was higher in people with dementia (IR 100.3 vs 76.4 per 100,000 person-months; HR 1.48 at 6 months post-diagnosis).
  • Risk was highest for unintentional poisonings, toxic pharmaceutical effects, and adverse drug reactions, particularly in the first 6 months after diagnosis.
  • Psychotropics, antithrombotics, and cardiovascular drugs were the medication classes most frequently implicated.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights a critical early post-diagnosis window for medication-related harm in dementia, supporting NHS medication review practices (e.g., structured medication reviews, deprescribing) around the time of diagnosis to reduce adverse drug events.
Limitations: Registry-based ICD-10 coding may underestimate or misclassify poisoning events, and residual confounding (e.g., frailty, polypharmacy severity) cannot be excluded.

Age and ageing

Effect of aspirin on healthspan in community-dwelling older adults: the ASPirin in reducing events in the elderly study

Zheng HT, Phyo AZZ, Wu Z et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Secondary analysis of a randomised controlled trial (ASPREE)
Key Question: Does daily low-dose aspirin reduce loss of healthspan (first occurrence of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, dementia, cancer, or death) in healthy older adults?
Key Findings:
  • 19,114 participants followed for a median 4.7 years; 30.5% experienced healthspan loss.
  • Event rates were near-identical between groups (82.1 vs 82.7 per 1000 person-years, aspirin vs placebo).
  • No significant benefit of aspirin (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.92–1.05); older age, male sex, smoking, hypertension, and frailty were associated with higher risk of healthspan loss.
Clinical Relevance: Reinforces current UK/NHS guidance against routine aspirin use for primary prevention in older adults, supporting a shift towards lifestyle-based strategies to preserve healthspan.
Limitations: As a composite outcome analysis, it cannot determine aspirin's differential effects on individual disease components, and findings apply to a relatively healthy trial population, limiting generalisability to frailer NHS patients.

Age and ageing

Costs and cost-effectiveness of palliative care in residential aged care homes: a scoping review

Yang X, Liu F, MacAndrew M et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Scoping review
Key Question: What is the existing evidence on the costs and cost-effectiveness of palliative care delivered in residential aged care (RAC) homes?
Key Findings:
  • 15 studies met inclusion criteria; economic outcomes fell into three categories: care delivery costs (n=3), healthcare system costs (n=10), and cost-effectiveness (n=2).
  • Palliative care was consistently associated with additional direct delivery costs, but 6/10 healthcare system cost studies showed overall cost reductions (one showed increased costs, three mixed, varying by time horizon/care continuity).
  • Both full economic evaluations suggested favourable cost-effectiveness versus usual care, though statistical significance was inconsistent.
Clinical Relevance: With NHS and UK care home providers facing pressure to justify palliative care investment amid constrained social care budgets, this review highlights potential system-level savings but underscores the weak evidence base for commissioning decisions.
Limitations: The evidence base is small, methodologically heterogeneous, and lacks robust full economic evaluations incorporating patient-centred outcomes, limiting generalisable conclusions.

A meta-analytical umbrella review assessing urban environment exposures and cognitive health outcomes

Glover S, Cleland C, Trott M et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Umbrella review (meta-analytical synthesis of systematic reviews)
Key Question: Which urban environmental exposures are most strongly linked to cognitive decline or dementia risk in ageing populations?
Key Findings:
  • PM2.5 exposure showed highly probable detrimental cognitive effects (10/11 significant outcomes); NO2/NOx showed weaker but notable associations (5/10 significant).
  • Social relationships were probably protective against cognitive decline (5/8 significant beneficial outcomes).
  • Green/blue space exposure was associated with reduced dementia risk (3/6 significant outcomes); travel behaviour had no identifiable evidence base.
Clinical Relevance: Supports UK public health and urban planning efforts (e.g., Clean Air Zones, social prescribing, green space access) as dementia-prevention strategies relevant to geriatric medicine and healthy ageing policy.
Limitations: Umbrella review is constrained by the quality and heterogeneity of included systematic reviews, limiting causal inference despite grading of evidence strength.

Age and ageing

Cognitive skills for daily decision making on admission is the most important factor associated with return to home or retirement home in a general-population low intensity rehab programme

Campbell TM, Dunlop N · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Retrospective cohort study
Key Question: Which admission characteristics predict discharge to home/retirement home versus long-term care following low-intensity rehabilitation (LIR) in older adults?
Key Findings:
  • Of 1662 LIR admissions, 54.8% were discharged home/to retirement home; 45.2% transitioned to long-term care.
  • Cognitive ability for daily decision-making was the strongest independent predictor of home/RH discharge (adjusted OR 1.455, 95% CI 1.209–1.751), even after adjusting for age, weight, rehab intensity, LoS, and ADLs.
  • Outcome gradient was marked: 68.3% with highest cognitive scores returned home/RH versus only 12.5% with lowest scores.
Clinical Relevance: For UK MDTs assessing rehab pathway suitability, admission cognitive screening (using routinely collected MDS-type data) could help target LIR resources and set realistic discharge expectations, relevant given expanding LIR provision amid an ageing NHS population.
Limitations: Retrospective design limits causal inference and may be affected by unmeasured confounders influencing discharge destination decisions.

Age and ageing

Prevalence of sarcopenia and individual- and country-level risk factors in 36 countries: pooled analysis of nationally representative individual data

Huang S, Lai W, Flood D et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Cross-sectional pooled analysis of individual-level data from 10 nationally representative ageing cohorts (multi-country).
Key Question: What is the global prevalence of sarcopenia in adults aged ≥50, and which individual- and country-level factors predict risk?
Key Findings:
  • Age- and gender-standardised sarcopenia prevalence ranged from 2.1% to 17.4% across 36 countries; ~92.7 million of 1.07 billion adults ≥50 affected.
  • Individual risk factors: male sex (RR 1.47, 95% CI 1.32–1.64), older age, lower education/wealth, unemployment, smoking, physical inactivity, ≥2 chronic diseases.
  • Country-level: higher GDP per capita strongly protective (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.30–0.60); higher Gender Development Index protective, higher labour force participation associated with increased risk.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights sarcopenia as a globally variable, socioeconomically patterned condition, reinforcing the need for UK geriatric services to consider deprivation and modifiable lifestyle factors alongside frailty/sarcopenia screening (e.g., via NHS Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment) rather than treating it as a purely biological ageing phenomenon.
Limitations: Cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding individual- and country-level associations.

Hospital at Home-What does the evidence tell us?

Vardy ERLC, Masoli JAH · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Editorial/commentary introducing a themed journal collection.
Key Question: What does current evidence show about the effectiveness and implementation of Hospital at Home (HaH) models for older adults?
Key Findings:
  • HaH models vary widely (admission avoidance, early supported discharge, CGA-based multidisciplinary care) but generally achieve outcomes comparable to inpatient care.
  • Evidence supports cost-effectiveness, reduced care home admissions, improved patient experience, and increased time spent living at home.
  • Success depends on multidisciplinary expertise, service coordination, and informal caregiver input, not technology alone; workforce capacity, digital exclusion, and caregiver burden remain key challenges.
Clinical Relevance: With NHS HaH services expanding rapidly post-pandemic and older people the predominant users, geriatricians need to understand implementation complexities to design safe, equitable, and sustainable services locally. *(No limitations section provided—this is an editorial summarising a collection, not primary research.)*

Age and ageing

Blood pressure polygenic score, cardiorespiratory fitness and odds of dementia: the HUNT Study

Lerfald M, Øvretveit K, Nilsen TIL et al. · 2026 Jul 02
Study Type: Prospective population-based cohort study
Key Question: Does genetic predisposition to high systolic blood pressure increase dementia risk, and does this differ by sex or cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)?
Key Findings:
  • In 9145 adults ≥70 years (HUNT4 70+), females in the highest quintile of BP polygenic score had higher odds of dementia (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.10–1.90); no association was seen in males (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.77–1.37).
  • Among females, the association was stronger in those with low CRF (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.08–2.17) versus high CRF (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.78–1.63), suggesting possible effect modification.
  • Dementia prevalence was 16.2% in females and 13.8% in males.
Clinical Relevance: Highlights a sex-specific genetic BP–dementia link and supports promoting cardiorespiratory fitness as a potentially modifiable risk-reduction strategy, relevant to UK dementia prevention and healthy ageing initiatives targeting older women.
Limitations: Cross-sectional dementia ascertainment within a cohort design limits causal inference regarding CRF's modifying effect.

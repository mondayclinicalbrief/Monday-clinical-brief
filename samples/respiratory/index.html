American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine

Reduced desaturation-based detection of sleep apnea events in self-identified Black individuals across two cohorts

Azarbarzin A, Sands SA, Messineo L et al. · 2026 Nov 04
Study Type: Secondary analysis of two prospective cohorts (MESA and SHHS) in participants free of baseline cardiovascular disease.
Key Question: Does pulse oximetry-based desaturation scoring under-detect obstructive respiratory events in Black individuals, and can heart-rate response serve as an oximetry-independent alternative for risk stratification?
Key Findings:
  • Black participants had ~20% fewer airflow-defined events meeting the ≥4% desaturation criterion than White participants (MESA ratio 0.79 [0.68–0.93]; SHHS 0.80 [0.68–0.94]); no other racial/ethnic group differed from White participants.
  • Heart-rate-based event detection (AHI-ΔHR6) was similar between Black and White participants. Desaturation-based AHI was not significantly associated with MACE/mortality in Black participants in either cohort.
  • AHI-ΔHR6 predicted MACE/mortality in both MESA (HR 1.33 [1.09–1.63]) and SHHS (HR 1.26 [1.02–1.55]).
Clinical Relevance: Desaturation-dependent AHI may under-diagnose and under-stratify OSA in Black patients, which matters for NHS sleep services using oximetry-based home studies and hypopnoea criteria.
Limitations: Skin pigmentation was not measured directly (self-identified race used as proxy), so attribution of findings to oximetry bias is inferential.

American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine

Development and Validation of a Pre-COPD Score to Predict Incident COPD

Çolak Y, Bhatt SP, Kinney GL et al. · 2026 Nov 06
Study Type: Prognostic model development and external validation (prospective population-based cohorts: CGPS, CCHS, COPDGene)
Key Question: Can a simple clinical score identify individuals without airflow obstruction who are at high risk of developing COPD?
Key Findings:
  • Score assigns points for smoking history ≥10 pack-years, active smoking, any respiratory symptom, asthma, and FEV1/FVC 0.70–0.75 (1 each), plus 4 points for FEV1 <80% predicted (PRISm).
  • Incident COPD occurred in 4.6% (CGPS), 7.9% (CCHS) and 12% (COPDGene). In CGPS, 10-year incidence rose from 1.5% (low risk) to 42% (very high risk); 5-year incidence in COPDGene rose from 4.1% to 31%.
  • AUC was 0.88 (CGPS, development), 0.76 (CCHS) and 0.81 (COPDGene); higher scores were also associated with exacerbations.
Clinical Relevance: The score uses routinely available history and spirometry, so it could help target case-finding and early intervention (e.g., smoking cessation) among high-risk people without obstruction, relevant to UK primary and secondary care pathways.
Limitations: Validation cohorts are predominantly North European/US research populations, and the outcome relied on a single pre-bronchodilator spirometry definition of COPD, limiting generalisability to UK practice; no evidence is given that acting on the score improves outcomes.

European Respiratory Journal

Major bleeding in patients with acute pulmonary embolism: results from the COPE study

Becattini C, Cimini LA, Vatrano M et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Prospective multicentre cohort study (COPE), n=5213
Key Question: What are the independent predictors of early and late major bleeding in acute PE, and how do bleeding-phenotypes compare with existing bleeding risk scores?
Key Findings:
  • 30-day ISTH major bleeding occurred in 3.1% (159/5213): 78 early (<7 days), 81 late (≥7 days). Rates were 12.9% with thrombolysis versus 2.5% without.
  • Active cancer, previous bleeding, dementia, anaemia, syncope and thrombolysis independently predicted early and 30-day MB; late MB was predicted only by active cancer, previous bleeding and anaemia.
  • Four phenotypes (previous VTE/oestrogen; unprovoked PE; cancer; fragile/comorbid) showed rising bleeding risk from #1 to #4, with discrimination comparable to PE-SARD, BACS and VTE-Bleed.
Clinical Relevance: Supports differentiated bleeding-risk assessment early versus later in PE management, and offers a pragmatic phenotype-based alternative to scores when making anticoagulation and thrombolysis decisions.
Limitations: The abstract gives no effect sizes, confidence intervals or discrimination statistics, so the claimed comparability of phenotypes with existing scores cannot be assessed from the abstract alone.

American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine

As-needed inhaled corticosteroids in asthma: an Official American Thoracic Society clinical practice guideline

Khurana S, Jenkins AR, Reddel HK et al. · 2026 Nov 03
Study Type: Clinical practice guideline (American Thoracic Society), based on systematic reviews and GRADE appraisal across six PICO questions.
Key Question: Should adolescents and adults with asthma use ICS-containing reliever therapy (ICS-formoterol or ICS+SABA) rather than SABA alone or SABA reliever with scheduled ICS-based maintenance?
Key Findings:
  • Steps 1-2: strong recommendation for as-needed-only ICS-formoterol or ICS-SABA over SABA-only; conditional recommendation over scheduled ICS plus as-needed SABA.
  • Steps 3-5: strong recommendation for MART with ICS-formoterol over scheduled ICS-LABA plus SABA reliever.
  • Steps 3-5 on scheduled ICS-LABA: conditional recommendation for ICS+SABA over SABA-only reliever.
  • Effect sizes and certainty-of-evidence ratings are not given in the abstract.
Clinical Relevance: The recommendations broadly align with GINA and the BTS/NICE/SIGN guideline, supporting the move away from SABA-only reliever in UK practice.
Limitations: US-framed (NAEPP steps); ICS+SABA recommendations may have limited applicability in UK practice, where combination ICS-SABA inhalers are not routinely available.

European Respiratory Journal

2026 Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) strategy report: executive summary and key changes

Reddel HK, Allan K, Bacharier LB et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Guideline executive summary (annual update of the GINA Strategy Report); not original research.
Key Question: What are the current evidence-based recommendations for asthma diagnosis, assessment, treatment and prevention across the lifespan, and what has changed for 2026?
Key Findings:
  • ICS-containing treatment is strongly recommended for all adults, adolescents, children and most preschoolers; SABA-only treatment is recommended against.
  • For adults and adolescents, Track 1 (low-dose ICS-formoterol as anti-inflammatory reliever, either AIR-only or MART) remains preferred owing to reduced severe exacerbations versus SABA-based regimens.
  • 2026 updates: expanded AIR options for adults and children, revised severe asthma and biologic selection guidance, and substantially updated acute asthma management, including post-discharge review.
Clinical Relevance: Provides the international benchmark against which UK practice (e.g. BTS/NICE/SIGN guidance) can be compared, particularly regarding AIR and MART use and biologic selection in severe asthma.
Limitations: The abstract gives no effect sizes, evidence grades or detail on the specific changes, so the full report is needed to assess their impact on UK practice.

American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine

Dupilumab is associated with improved lung functions in patients with COPD and type 2 inflammation: a post hoc analysis of the BOREAS and NOTUS trials

Han MK, Rabe KF, Sciurba FC et al. · 2026 Oct 30
Study Type: Post hoc pooled analysis of two phase 3 RCTs (BOREAS and NOTUS)
Key Question: Does dupilumab improve spirometric lung function in patients with COPD and type 2 inflammation, and in which subgroups?
Key Findings:
  • Versus placebo, dupilumab improved pre-bronchodilator FEV1 by 83 mL (95% CI 53–112) and post-bronchodilator FEV1 by 71 mL (41–101) at week 12, with effects sustained to week 52. FVC, FEV1/FVC and FEF25–75% also improved.
  • Benefit was seen irrespective of smoking status or coexisting emphysema, and was greater with higher baseline eosinophils or FeNO.
  • The proportion achieving >100 mL pre-bronchodilator FEV1 gain was 42.2% vs 31.1% at week 12, and 36.9% vs 31.2% at week 52.
Clinical Relevance: Supports dupilumab as a biologic option for eosinophilic COPD with exacerbations despite triple therapy, and suggests eosinophils and FeNO may help identify those most likely to gain lung function.
Limitations: Post hoc analysis, so subgroup findings are hypothesis-generating; the mean FEV1 differences are modest, and the proportion achieving >100 mL gain narrowed substantially by week 52.

American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine

Lung Allograft Function After Primary Graft Dysfunction in the Lung Transplant Outcomes Group Cohort

Pandya K, Gallop R, Localio AR et al. · 2026 Oct 30
Study Type: Multicentre prospective cohort study (8 centres, 2007–2018; n=2,419 lung transplant recipients)
Key Question: Does primary graft dysfunction (PGD) worsen long-term allograft function, either through reduced peak lung function or increased chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD)?
Key Findings:
  • PGD (n=510) was associated with lower maximum potential FEV1 (−311 ml, 95% CI −245 to −377) and a 12.5% lower probability of reaching GLI-predicted FEV1 (95% CI −17 to −8%).
  • 5-year CLAD cumulative incidence did not differ by PGD (−2.2%, 95% CI −6.4 to 1.9%), accounting for death as a competing risk.
  • No difference in obstructive or restrictive CLAD phenotypes, and no effect modification by age, sex, BMI, diagnosis, or transplant type.
Clinical Relevance: PGD appears to cap achievable lung function rather than accelerate CLAD, which may inform post-transplant counselling and interpretation of lower plateau spirometry in UK transplant follow-up.
Limitations: Observational design leaves potential residual confounding, and the findings derive from a North American cohort, which may limit generalisability to UK practice.

Pretransplant Pulmonary Hemodynamics, Explant Histology, and Primary Graft Dysfunction after Lung Transplantation for Silicosis

Mendoza MA, Tang A, Marchevsky AM et al. · 2026 Nov 05
Study Type: Retrospective single-centre cohort study (prospectively enrolled participants), with descriptive histopathological comparison against PAH explants.
Key Question: What is the prevalence and pathological basis of pulmonary hypertension in silicosis-associated progressive massive fibrosis (PMF) patients undergoing lung transplantation, and how does it relate to primary graft dysfunction (PGD)?
Key Findings:
  • PH was present in >95% of 21 transplanted PMF patients, with invasive haemodynamics resembling those of patients transplanted for PAH.
  • Explants showed fibrotic obliteration and extrinsic perivascular narrowing of vessels; classic PAH-type muscular hypertrophy and plexiform lesions were uncommon.
  • Exploratory analysis: pretransplant RV dysfunction was more frequent in patients developing PGD (71% vs 21%; risk difference 0.50, 95% CI 0.06–0.75); TAPSE/RVSP ratio was lower (0.27 vs 0.39; g −0.93, 95% CI −1.88 to 0.02).
Clinical Relevance: As artificial stone silicosis cases emerge in the UK, clinicians assessing these patients for transplant should anticipate PH and RV dysfunction, which may inform risk stratification for PGD.
Limitations: Very small single-centre sample with exploratory, unadjusted comparisons (CI for TAPSE/RVSP crosses zero), precluding firm conclusions on PGD predictors.
